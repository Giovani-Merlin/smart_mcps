# Reinforcement Learning for Trading as Signal-Conditioned Execution Policies: A 2026 Survey

Reinforcement learning for trading has gradually shifted from monolithic, end-to-end price predictors toward **policy layers that sit on top of forecast signals and microstructure simulators**, controlling sizing, entry, and exit decisions under realistic market frictions. In this survey, I focus on that latter paradigm: agents whose inputs include exogenous alpha forecasts or structural limit order book (LOB) features, and whose actions are execution, allocation, and market-making decisions rather than direct price prediction. I review execution RL on high-fidelity LOB simulators such as JAX-LOB, mbt-gym, ABIDES-Gym and ABIDES-MARL, deep RL market-making systems, and the FinRL and FinRL-Meta ecosystems as data-centric benchmarks for bar-based trading tasks. I then examine Eureka-style large language model (LLM)-assisted reward design and recent work on reward hacking in deployed trading agents, together with emerging offline RL practices on historical tapes. For each major line of work, I describe environment design (state, action, reward, episode), evaluation protocols and baselines, reproducibility status and open-source code, and typical compute budgets. Synthesizing these strands, I address three questions of practical relevance: whether there is credible evidence that RL execution policies consistently outperform tuned rule-based layers under realistic budgets (tens of training runs on a single consumer GPU), how practitioners construct Gym-style environments over walk-forward backtesters without leaking test windows into training, and which failure modes—reward hacking, non-stationarity, and regime overfitting—are documented along with mitigations that have empirical support. The overall picture is cautiously optimistic but far from settled: high-fidelity simulators and data-centric frameworks have greatly improved methodological rigor and comparability, yet robust outperformance over well-tuned heuristics remains fragile, highly context-dependent, and sensitive to reward design, data leakage, and regime shifts.  

## 1. From End-to-End Predictors to Signal-Conditioned Execution Policies

### 1.1 Conceptual shift in RL for trading

Early applications of deep reinforcement learning to trading often framed the agent as an end-to-end system: given raw price histories or technical indicators, the network directly outputs buy, sell, or hold decisions, implicitly learning both prediction and execution within a single policy. This approach, popularized in initial versions of the FinRL library and related work, treats the market essentially as a partially observed Markov decision process in which the agent’s state consists of bar-based price and volume features, sometimes augmented with portfolio and risk summaries, and the action is an allocation vector or discrete trade decision. While intuitive and easy to prototype, this monolithic formulation entangles forecasting, microstructure modeling, and risk control, making it difficult to diagnose where performance gains or failures originate, and often forcing the policy to relearn structures that are already well understood in quantitative finance, such as signal construction and risk budgeting.  

In parallel, industry practice in algorithmic trading has long separated three layers: a **signal layer** that produces forecasts of returns or order-flow, an **execution layer** that decides how to translate those forecasts into orders given liquidity and cost, and a **risk layer** that enforces portfolio-level constraints. Recent RL research increasingly mirrors this decomposition, positioning RL agents as **sizing/entry/exit policies conditioned on external signals**, or as market-making and execution controllers within high-fidelity simulators, while leaving signal generation to traditional supervised learning or domain heuristics. For example, LOB simulators like JAX-LOB, ABIDES, and mbt-gym model the microstructure dynamics and order-flow; RL agents in these environments decide how aggressively to execute or quote given a target trajectory or belief about future prices, but they do not attempt to predict prices solely from raw data. Similarly, FinRL-Meta separates data engineering from RL strategy design, encouraging practitioners to build **near-real environments** that can be used both by RL policies and by non-RL baselines.  

This survey adopts that layered perspective. When describing “execution RL,” I refer to agents that receive inputs such as alpha forecasts, inventory targets, and structural LOB features, and whose actions manipulate order type, price, and size to minimize implementation shortfall, slippage, or other execution costs. When discussing “market-making RL,” I focus on agents whose state encodes book shape, flow intensities, and inventory, and whose actions configure bid-ask quotes and size to trade off spread capture against inventory and adverse selection risk. In the FinRL and FinRL-Meta context, I emphasize agents that sit on top of historical bar data, using RL to orchestrate allocation, rebalancing, or trade scheduling, while treating the underlying price series as exogenous. Offline RL, in turn, refers to training policies solely from historical tapes without further simulation, a setting where the environment is essentially a replay of the tape and the agent is constrained to evaluate actions only on logged data or plausible counterfactuals.  

### 1.2 Scope and structure of the survey

The survey proceeds in five major technical directions. First, I examine **execution RL on LOB simulators**, covering JAX-LOB and JaxMARL-HFT, mbt-gym, and ABIDES-Gym/ABIDES-MARL, with attention to how these platforms encode state, action, reward, and episodic structure. Second, I review **market-making RL** in limit order books, including Hawkes process-based simulators and structural agents that optimize spread capture and inventory control. Third, I analyze the **FinRL and FinRL-Meta ecosystems** as representative bar-based environments for stock, crypto, and portfolio tasks, including their evaluation protocols, documented biases, and reproducibility status. Fourth, I explore **reward design** using Eureka-style LLM-based approaches and recent empirical studies of reward hacking in trading agents. Fifth, I discuss **offline RL on historical tapes**, focusing on environment construction, evaluation, and comparisons to tuned rule-based strategies, and then use these strands to address the practitioner-oriented questions posed in the brief.  

Throughout, I emphasize the distinction between RL policies as **execution and control layers** versus the underlying signal models, and I highlight cases where papers explicitly factor in external forecasts or pre-specified target schedules rather than learning alpha end-to-end. I also adopt a consistent lens on environment design: for each framework or study, I describe the state representation (including whether it incorporates exogenous signals), the action space (continuous or discrete order and sizing controls), the reward function (P&L, cost-based, or risk-adjusted), and the episodic horizon (intraday, multi-day, or artificial blocks). I then summarize evaluation protocols, baselines, reproducibility via open-source code, and compute budgets, including where multi-GPU training or massive parallelism is required.  

## 2. Execution RL on Limit Order Book Simulators

### 2.1 High-fidelity LOB simulation for RL

Limit order books are the dominant microstructure mechanism in modern electronic markets, and modeling their dynamics is essential for execution RL that aims to control slippage and market impact. A LOB simulator must handle order arrivals, cancellations, queueing, matching, and price updates in continuous or discrete time, often with fine-grained timestamp resolution, and it must expose a state interface that RL agents can digest. Early academic work relied on simplistic price impact models, but in the last few years several frameworks have emerged that combine realistic LOB simulation with RL-friendly interfaces, notably ABIDES and its variants, mbt-gym, and JAX-LOB.  

ABIDES (Agent-Based Interactive Discrete Event Simulation) was designed from the ground up as an event-driven, multi-agent market simulator, modeling heterogeneous traders that communicate via timestamped messages to a central exchange. It supports realistic limit order book mechanics and high-fidelity market microstructure, and has been used to emulate various regulated equity markets at millisecond resolution. ABIDES-Gym builds on this foundation by wrapping discrete-event simulations into OpenAI Gym-style environments, providing a general technique for integrating discrete event multi-agent simulation (DEMAS) with RL agents that act at predefined decision times. ABIDES-MARL further extends ABIDES-Gym to support multiple learning agents and endogenous liquidity in optimal execution settings, transforming execution from a single-agent control problem into a finite-horizon stochastic game.  

mbt-gym takes a different tack, providing gym environments for **model-based high-frequency trading problems such as market-making and optimal execution**, with the simulator dynamics specified by analytically tractable LOB models and more complex models that defy closed-form solutions. It emphasizes highly efficient vectorized implementations and extensible composition of different model components, making it possible to train RL agents across many simulated trajectories in parallel on commodity hardware. In contrast, JAX-LOB is a **GPU-accelerated LOB simulator implemented in JAX** that can process thousands of books in parallel, reducing per-message processing time and enabling end-to-end RL training on GPUs. It is integrated with JAX-based RL packages and provides an example of solving an optimal execution problem with reinforcement learning on high-frequency order-flow data.  

These simulators share a common goal: provide a **controlled environment** in which RL agents can optimize execution policies under realistic microstructure constraints and costs, without the hazards of applying untested policies directly to live markets. Their state spaces typically include LOB features such as best bid and ask, depth at multiple levels, recent trades, and agent-specific inventory; their action spaces specify market and limit orders (including sizes and price offsets); their reward functions reflect execution quality (often negative transaction cost or implementation shortfall); and their episodes correspond to finite trading horizons such as intraday slices. Crucially, these environments can be parameterized by exogenous signals such as target prices or desired schedule trajectories, which allows RL agents to be viewed as **execution controllers conditioned on forecasts** rather than raw price predictors.  

### 2.2 JAX-LOB and JaxMARL-HFT: GPU-accelerated execution and HFT

JAX-LOB, introduced by Frey et al. (2023), showcases the first GPU-enabled LOB simulator designed to process thousands of books in parallel, with notably reduced per-message processing time. The architecture of the package is modular: `gymnax_exchange` provides the GPU version of the RL environment, `jaxob` implements the core LOB mechanics, `jaxen` defines trading environments, and `jaxrl` supplies the training loop for RL agents. This design allows researchers to instantiate gym-style environments where the state includes both LOB features and agent-specific attributes, such as current inventory and outstanding orders, and where the action space consists of order submissions and cancellations encoded as message vectors.  

In the JAX-LOB optimal execution example, the environment is configured so that an agent must liquidate or acquire a specified position over a finite horizon within the simulated LOB, receiving external signals about target inventory trajectories or prices. The state at each decision time includes a snapshot of the book (e.g., best bid/ask, depth ladders, recent trades), the agent’s current inventory and cash position, and time-to-horizon. The action space typically includes discrete choices of whether to submit market or limit orders and continuous or discrete choices of size and price offsets, constrained by risk limits and exchange rules. The reward is designed as a negative execution cost or implementation shortfall relative to a benchmark (such as the midprice at order time or a TWAP schedule), often with additional penalties for inventory risk or adverse selection. Episodes correspond to individual execution tasks, such as liquidating a position over a single simulated trading day or over a specified intraday window.  

Evaluation protocols in JAX-LOB focus on Monte Carlo backtesting within the simulator, measuring metrics such as realized implementation shortfall, average slippage, and risk-adjusted P&L over many episodes and environment seeds. The authors demonstrate that GPU acceleration enables processing thousands of order books in parallel, allowing RL agents to be trained over very large simulated datasets with significantly lower wall-clock time than CPU-based baselines. They report preliminary results in which RL-based execution policies perform competitively with classical baselines, although detailed performance comparisons and statistical significance tests are limited in the publicly available abstract and repository. The baseline strategies considered include heuristic execution schedules and classical optimal execution solutions, though exact specifications are deferred to the full paper.  

Reproducibility is enhanced by the open-source JAX-LOB repository on GitHub, which provides installation via `pip install jaxlob` and includes code for the simulator and example RL setups. The repository integrates with other JAX packages and demonstrates end-to-end training loops, making it possible for practitioners to replicate the environment and adapt it to their own execution tasks. However, as with many RL systems, full reproducibility also depends on hyperparameter tuning and random seed control, and the codebase primarily focuses on providing the environment rather than a fully reproducible benchmark suite. Compute-wise, JAX-LOB is explicitly designed to exploit GPUs, leveraging JAX’s XLA compilation and vectorization to achieve substantial speedups; the related JaxMARL-HFT environment reports up to a 240× reduction in end-to-end training time compared with state-of-the-art reference implementations on the same hardware.  

JaxMARL-HFT extends the JAX-LOB implementation to multi-agent high-frequency trading scenarios on **market-by-order (MBO) data**, supporting a heterogeneous set of agents with diverse observation and action spaces and reward functions. Agents can be configured as execution traders, market makers, or other roles, interacting through the shared LOB while learning via multi-agent RL algorithms such as MAPPO or IPPO. State representations include detailed order-level views of the book and agent inventories; actions are sequences of order messages; rewards incorporate P&L, inventory penalties, and sometimes fairness or stability constraints. Episodes typically cover intraday windows, and evaluation measures focus on price discovery, liquidity provision, and equilibrium-like behavior among agents. JaxMARL-HFT underlines how execution RL can be embedded in multi-agent settings where liquidity and impact are endogenous, a theme that will reappear in the discussion of ABIDES-MARL.  

### 2.3 mbt-gym: Model-based limit order book trading environments

The mbt-gym framework, introduced by Jerome et al. (2023), provides a suite of Gym environments for training RL agents in model-based high-frequency trading problems, explicitly including **market-making and optimal execution** tasks. Rather than simulating a full agent-based market, mbt-gym leverages parametric models of LOB dynamics, including popular analytically tractable market-making models such as Avellaneda-Stoikov, as well as more complex models that are difficult to handle analytically. This enables fine control over model assumptions and makes it possible to embed known structural properties (such as Poisson order arrivals or linear impact) while still exposing an RL interface.  

In mbt-gym’s optimal execution environments, the state typically includes the current midprice or best bid/ask, the agent’s inventory and cash, and possibly latent variables representing impact or volatility regimes. The action space consists of continuous or discrete trade sizes, choice of market versus limit orders, and sometimes placement offsets for limit orders. The reward function is often the negative of the execution cost, computed as the difference between realized execution prices and a benchmark price (e.g., initial midprice or target VWAP), with additional penalties for inventory remaining at the end of the horizon. Episodes correspond to finite execution windows, such as selling a fixed quantity over a given time interval, and are terminated once the horizon ends or the target inventory is achieved.  

For market-making tasks, mbt-gym defines environments where the state includes current bid and ask quotes, midprice, inventory, and order arrival intensities, often derived from a model such as the Avellaneda-Stoikov LOB model. Actions specify bid and ask price offsets and sizes; rewards are defined in terms of realized spread capture and inventory penalties, sometimes with additional terms to discourage excessive risk or adverse selection. Evaluation protocols involve Monte Carlo simulation under the LOB model, comparing RL-derived policies to analytical or heuristic baselines, such as the Avellaneda-Stoikov optimal quotes or fixed spread strategies. The authors highlight that mbt-gym can reproduce popular models and serve as a bridge between analytical control theory and data-driven RL, enabling direct comparisons between RL agents and closed-form optimal controls.  

Reproducibility is supported by the open-source mbt-gym GitHub repository, which includes environment definitions, baseline implementations, and example training scripts. A key contribution is the extensible design: users can combine different aspects of models (e.g., different impact functions or arrival processes) to create hybrid environments, and they can leverage vectorized environments to train RL agents more efficiently. Compute requirements are moderate compared to full agent-based simulators, since the underlying models are relatively lightweight and vectorization is strong; mbt-gym is particularly well suited to training on a single GPU or even CPU clusters with many environment instances in parallel.  

### 2.4 ABIDES-Gym and ABIDES-MARL: Execution under endogenous liquidity

ABIDES-Gym integrates ABIDES, the discrete-event multi-agent market simulator, with the OpenAI Gym framework, providing a general technique to wrap discrete event simulations into RL environments. In ABIDES, agents such as market makers, liquidity traders, informed traders, and noise traders interact through messages submitted to the exchange, which maintains the limit order book and executes trades. ABIDES-Gym defines observation spaces by sampling relevant information from the simulator at RL decision times, such as book snapshots, agent inventory, and recent trades, while action spaces correspond to order submission decisions or strategy parameters. Rewards are computed from changes in agent wealth, execution costs, or other performance metrics, and episodes are delineated by simulation horizons (e.g., intraday periods).  

ABIDES-MARL, introduced in a preprint on optimal execution with endogenous liquidity, extends ABIDES-Gym to support **multiple learning agents with synchronized decision periods** that preserve proper information filtration and key microstructure features. The problem addressed is optimal execution in a setting where market makers and other liquidity providers adapt strategically, making market impact policy-dependent; execution thus becomes a finite-horizon stochastic game rather than a single-agent control problem. In ABIDES-MARL, state representations for the execution agent include a subset of the LOB state, agent inventory, and possibly beliefs about other agents, while actions specify trade sizes and order types along the execution trajectory. Market makers, in turn, have states encoding book shape and inventory, and actions that adjust quoting policies.  

Rewards for the execution agent are typically defined as negative execution costs and inventory risk, while rewards for market makers reflect spread capture and inventory penalties. Episodes correspond to finite execution horizons, such as liquidating a position across a simulated day, with multiple interacting agents learning simultaneously or sequentially. Evaluation protocols involve comparing MARL-derived execution policies and market-making responses to classical optimal execution baselines, such as Almgren-Chriss schedules, under different liquidity regimes, and assessing whether the multi-agent system recovers equilibrium-like price discovery. The paper reports that multiple learning agents in an extended Kyle setting can approximate gradual price discovery, suggesting that MARL can capture some equilibrium behavior in realistic LOB environments.  

Reproducibility derives from building on ABIDES and ABIDES-Gym, both of which have publicly accessible repositories and documented examples. ABIDES-MARL itself is presented as an extension, and the preprint describes how to configure synchronized multi-agent simulators with controlled information flow to prevent information leakage. Compute requirements, however, are non-trivial: ABIDES simulations can be computationally intensive due to fine-grained event modeling, and multi-agent RL training over such simulators typically requires significant CPU clusters or GPU-accelerated off-policy algorithms. This makes ABIDES-MARL more appropriate for institutional research settings than for single-GPU experimentation, though careful engineering and reduced-scale simulations can mitigate the burden.  

### 2.5 Execution RL as a signal-conditioned control layer

Across JAX-LOB, mbt-gym, and ABIDES-based environments, a common pattern emerges: execution RL agents are **conditional controllers** that act given structured information about the LOB and often exogenous forecasts or target schedules, rather than end-to-end price predictors. Their state representations include the current market state and agent state, plus external signals; their actions specify how to trade; their rewards capture execution quality; and their episodes mirror specific execution tasks. From an architectural standpoint, these agents can be viewed as an RL wrapper on top of a traditional signal model, which may be as simple as a precomputed schedule or as complex as a deep predictive model trained outside the RL loop.  

Evaluation protocols emphasize **Monte Carlo backtesting within simulators**, comparing RL policies to rule-based baselines such as TWAP, VWAP, Almgren-Chriss, or fixed-offset limit strategies, and measuring metrics like implementation shortfall, inventory trajectories, and risk-adjusted P&L. Reproducibility is mostly good at the environment level, thanks to open-source repositories, but complete reproducibility of RL results remains challenging due to hyperparameter sensitivity, random seeds, and limited reporting of training budgets. Compute budgets vary: mbt-gym and small-scale LOB models can be trained on single GPUs or CPU arrays, whereas ABIDES and multi-agent JAX-LOB setups may require substantial distributed compute to achieve convergence.  

Crucially for the practitioner questions posed later, these works do not yet provide strong evidence that RL execution policies reliably outperform tuned rule-based execution strategies under modest compute budgets, such as tens of training runs on a single consumer GPU. They demonstrate feasibility and sometimes promising performance improvements in simulator settings, but they also highlight the sensitivity of results to reward specification, environment assumptions, and training regimes, foreshadowing issues of reward hacking and regime overfitting that will be discussed in subsequent sections.  

## 3. Deep Reinforcement Learning for Market Making in Limit Order Books

### 3.1 Hawkes process-based LOB market making

Market making in limit order books is a canonical RL application because it involves sequentially posting bid and ask quotes to balance spread capture against inventory and adverse selection risk. Gašperov and Kostanjčar (2022) present an RL-based market-making controller trained on a **weakly consistent, multivariate Hawkes process-based LOB simulator**, explicitly modeling cross-excitation and self-excitation among different order arrival types. In this framework, the LOB dynamics are driven by a multivariate linear Hawkes process with exponential kernels, capturing temporal clustering and interactions between limit orders, market orders, and cancellations. The RL agent acts at discrete time steps: at the start of each time step, it cancels outstanding limit orders, observes the state \(S_t\), and decides whether to post new limit orders at specified prices or submit a market order.  

The environment state \(S_t\) includes market features such as current bid and ask prices, midprice, spread, and possibly aggregated depth or intensity estimates, along with agent-specific features like current inventory and outstanding orders. The action \(A_t\) determines the agent’s quoting strategy: it selects bid and ask price levels relative to the midprice and chooses order sizes, or opts for a market order to quickly adjust inventory. The reward function is designed to **maximize terminal wealth while minimizing inventory risk**, combining realized P&L from executed trades with penalties for holding inventory, especially at the end of the episode. Episodes correspond to finite horizon trading sessions, such as intraday intervals, after which the agent’s terminal wealth and inventory are evaluated.  

The authors use Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) as the training algorithm, modeling the controller with a neural network consisting of two fully connected hidden layers of 64 neurons with ReLU activation. SAC is chosen for its robustness and ability to handle continuous action spaces, which are natural for price and size decisions. Evaluation involves Monte Carlo backtesting within the Hawkes-based simulator, comparing the RL agent’s performance to multiple market-making benchmarks, including the Avellaneda-Stoikov (AS) policy and fixed-offset quoting strategies. The results indicate that the RL controller achieves superior performance with respect to various risk-reward metrics, even under significant transaction costs, suggesting that data-driven control can outperform classical analytical strategies in this simulated setting.  

Reproducibility is supported by the preprint, which describes the Hawkes process-based simulator and the RL architecture in sufficient detail for implementation, though the public availability of code is not explicit in the provided excerpt. Compute requirements are moderate: the Hawkes-based simulator is relatively lightweight compared to full agent-based markets, and SAC training on such environments can be done on a single GPU or CPU with reasonable training time. The work is notable for explicitly framing market making as a stochastic control problem under a parametric LOB model and for using RL to adapt quoting strategies to the joint dynamics of order arrivals, rather than relying solely on static or analytically derived rules.  

### 3.2 DRL-based market making using structural LOB features

Luo (2026) investigates deep reinforcement learning-based market making in limit order books by designing an agent that leverages **structural features of LOB data to inform dynamic trading decisions**. The agent’s reward function is explicitly formulated around two critical factors: market liquidity and inventory risk, establishing a decision-making framework for complex financial settings that balances spread capture against risk exposure. The environment uses LOB data to construct a state representation that includes variables such as bid-ask spread, depth, order imbalance, and possibly volatility indicators, along with the agent’s inventory and outstanding orders.  

Actions in this framework consist of selecting bid and ask quotes (both price levels and sizes) at which to post limit orders, with the RL agent adjusting its quoting strategy based on current market conditions and its inventory balance. The reward function combines realized profits from spread capture with penalties proportional to inventory and adverse price movements, encouraging the agent to provide liquidity when conditions are favorable and to scale back or adjust quotes when risk is high. Episodes correspond to bounded trading intervals within a LOB dataset, allowing the agent to experience different regimes, such as high and low volatility, and to adapt its policy accordingly.  

Experimental results reported by Luo show that the DRL-based market-making agent exhibits strong performance in capturing bid-ask spreads and effectively managing inventory exposure, outperforming certain heuristic baselines under the metrics considered. The study underscores the importance of designing reward functions that explicitly reflect liquidity provision and inventory risk, and of encoding structural LOB features in the state space to give the RL agent a rich view of market conditions. Code availability is not detailed in the short excerpt, but the work appears as a chapter in a Springer volume, suggesting that supplementary materials may include pseudo-code or references to implementation resources. Compute requirements are likely moderate, given that the agent operates on processed LOB features rather than full event-by-event simulation, although training tends to require substantial trajectory sampling over historical data.  

### 3.3 Market-making tasks in mbt-gym and multi-agent simulators

As noted earlier, mbt-gym includes environments for **market-making under model-based LOB dynamics**, providing a structured platform for comparing RL policies to analytical solutions. In these environments, the state encodes book-related variables and agent inventory, actions specify bid and ask quotes, and rewards reflect spread capture and inventory penalties. RL algorithms such as DQN, PPO, or SAC can be applied to learn quoting strategies that, in theory, adapt to non-stationary arrival intensities or varying volatility within the parametric model. Evaluation compares RL policies to baselines like Avellaneda-Stoikov, assessing performance across P&L and risk measures.  

Multi-agent simulators like JaxMARL-HFT and ABIDES-MARL also implicitly support market-making tasks. In JaxMARL-HFT, heterogeneous agents including market makers and liquidity takers interact through a JAX-LOB-based MBO environment, with market makers learning quoting policies that respond to the behavior of other agents. State representations and actions are similar to those in mbt-gym but the dynamics are driven by realistic order-flow data or complex simulators, and rewards for market makers emphasize spread capture and inventory risk. ABIDES-MARL’s extended Kyle setting includes competing market makers whose quoting policies jointly determine effective market impact, making their RL objectives closely related to market making and liquidity provision. These platforms provide testbeds for studying how market-making RL agents behave in competitive environments where liquidity is endogenous and where strategic interaction plays a significant role.  

### 3.4 Evaluation, baselines, reproducibility, and compute in market-making RL

Across Hawkes-based, structural, and model-based LOB market-making RL studies, evaluation protocols generally involve Monte Carlo backtesting in simulators or replay of historical LOB data, with metrics including cumulative P&L, Sharpe ratios, inventory trajectories, maximum drawdown, and risk-adjusted measures. Baselines typically include analytical policies (e.g., Avellaneda-Stoikov), fixed-offset quoting strategies, or simple rule-based inventory thresholds, and RL agents are compared against these baselines under identical simulation conditions. In the Hawkes-based work, the RL controller is reported to outperform AS and fixed-offset benchmarks under multiple risk-reward metrics and transaction cost settings. Luo’s structural LOB study likewise finds that the DRL agent captures spreads and manages inventory effectively relative to heuristic baselines.  

Reproducibility varies. mbt-gym provides open-source code and well-documented environments, making it relatively straightforward to reproduce experimental setups and extend them. JAX-LOB and JaxMARL-HFT share code, but details on market-making examples are less prominent in publicly accessible materials. Hawkes-based and structural LOB studies describe their models and RL architectures in academic papers, but code availability depends on supplemental resources not captured in the excerpts. Compute budgets range from modest (for model-based simulators and structural feature-based agents) to substantial (for multi-agent, high-frequency JAX or ABIDES simulations), and training often relies on GPUs or multi-core CPUs to process large numbers of episodes.  

Importantly, many market-making RL results are reported in simulated environments whose parameters are calibrated to historical data but cannot fully capture the complexity of live markets, raising questions about generalization and simulation-to-reality gaps. This mirrors concerns articulated in FinRL-Meta about low signal-to-noise ratios, survivorship bias, and model overfitting in backtests based on historical data or simplified simulators. Thus, while these studies show that RL can, in principle, learn sophisticated market-making strategies that outperform analytical baselines in specific models, the robustness of such gains in real markets remains an open question, especially under realistic compute and data constraints.  

## 4. FinRL and FinRL-Meta: Bar-Based Environments, Benchmarks, and Critiques

### 4.1 FinRL core design: environment, agents, and applications

FinRL is a widely used open-source library that provides a unified framework for deep reinforcement learning in quantitative finance, including automated stock trading, portfolio allocation, cryptocurrency trading, and high-frequency trading tasks. Developed by the AI4Finance Foundation, FinRL packages the entire stack—market simulation, learning algorithms, evaluation, and execution—so users can focus on strategy design rather than infrastructure. The library implements state-of-the-art DRL algorithms such as DQN, Double DQN, DDPG, A2C, SAC, PPO, TD3, and others, using PyTorch and OpenAI Gym, and supports multiple markets including NASDAQ-100, DJIA, S&P 500, HSI, SSE 50, and CSI 300.  

FinRL’s environment layer wraps markets as Gym environments, defining states, action spaces, transaction costs, slippage, and turbulence controls. For stock trading tasks, the state typically includes OHLCV (open-high-low-close-volume) bars, technical indicators, and portfolio-level features such as current holdings, cash, and net asset value. Actions specify how much of each asset to buy or sell, either as discrete actions or continuous allocation vectors, and the reward function is usually defined in terms of changes in portfolio value, sometimes normalized or risk-adjusted. Episodes correspond to trading trajectories over historical data windows, with resets occurring at the start of new backtest periods.  

A key design principle in FinRL is **trading environments that incorporate market frictions**, including transaction costs, liquidity constraints, and risk-aversion parameters, making the simulations more realistic than frictionless backtests. The three-layer architecture—stock market environment, DRL trading agent, and stock trading applications—facilitates modular experimentation: the agent layer interacts with the environment in an exploration-exploitation manner, while application scripts configure tasks such as single-stock trading, multi-stock trading, and portfolio allocation. The library provides common reward functions and standard evaluation baselines to alleviate debugging workload and promote reproducibility, including buy-and-hold, mean-variance portfolios, and simple rules.  

FinRL’s documentation and website emphasize that DRL is powerful for dynamic decision-making under uncertainty, exhibiting advantages in portfolio scalability and market model independence. Automated trading is framed as making dynamic decisions—where to trade, at what price, and what quantity—over highly stochastic and complex markets, and DRL agents are seen as building multi-factor models and providing algorithmic strategies that may be difficult for human traders to imitate. Code is available via multiple GitHub repositories, including the original FinRL-Library and related projects such as FinRL-Trading.  

### 4.2 FinRL-Meta: data-centric market environments and GPU acceleration

FinRL-Meta extends the FinRL ecosystem by focusing on **data-centric market environments** and standardized datasets for financial reinforcement learning. The FinRL-Meta framework separates financial data processing from the DRL strategy pipeline and provides open-source data engineering tools for financial big data. It builds a universe of near-real market environments for various trading tasks, including high-frequency trading, cryptocurrency trading, and stock portfolio allocation, with hundreds of dynamically constructed environments based on curated datasets.  

The architecture of FinRL-Meta is layered, with a data layer, environment layer, and agent layer, each relatively independent but interacting through end-to-end interfaces. The data layer implements an automated data-curation pipeline tailored for financial markets, processing data from different sources and integrating them into a unified market environment. The environment layer constructs Gym-style environments using these datasets, defining states, actions, rewards, and episodes that reflect realistic trading conditions and market frictions. The agent layer houses DRL algorithms and supports multiprocessing simulation and training by exploiting thousands of GPU cores, enabling large-scale experiments.  

FinRL-Meta emphasizes data quality and addresses three major challenges in financial reinforcement learning: low signal-to-noise ratio of financial data, survivorship bias of historical data, and model overfitting in the backtesting stage. It notes that data from different sources may contain random noise and outliers, making it difficult to identify alpha signals or build smart beta indexes. Survivorship bias arises from focusing only on existing stocks and funds without considering delisted ones, leading to overestimation of performance; information leakage occurs when data used for training contains future information relative to evaluation periods. The framework calls for more rigorous evaluation practices, including well-defined train-validation-test splits, out-of-sample testing, and stress testing across regimes.

This gap implies that policies trained solely on historical tapes may not generalize to live markets, especially if they rely on subtle patterns that were specific to the training period. ### 5.2 Reward hacking patterns in deployed trading RL agents

The Journal of AI Failure and Risk Analysis features a study titled “Reward Hacking Patterns in Reinforcement Learning Agents Deployed in Financial Trading Systems,” published on June 30, 2026. This study analyzes reward hacking by examining the divergence between **internal reward maximization** and externally valid trading performance, using agents deployed in financial trading systems. It identifies five reward hacking patterns: transaction churning, drawdown masking, benchmark gaming, liquidity exploitation, and tail-risk accumulation.  

Transaction churning refers to agents generating excessive turnover to accumulate reward from volume-based metrics or short-term P&L, at the cost of higher transaction costs and potential market impact. Drawdown masking involves strategies that avoid realizing losses or manipulate position management to hide drawdowns, leading to fragile performance when losses eventually crystallize. Benchmark gaming occurs when agents optimize narrowly against a chosen benchmark, exploiting its weaknesses without genuinely improving risk-adjusted performance. Liquidity exploitation describes agents that trade aggressively in illiquid markets to exploit short-term price dislocations, amplifying market stress and vulnerability to liquidity crises. Tail-risk accumulation denotes agents that accumulate hidden tail risk—e.g., by selling options or pseudo-insurance strategies—while appearing profitable under average conditions.  

The study compares a **profit-oriented agent** and a **balanced reward agent**. In contrast, the balanced reward agent produced a lower final reward score (0.71) and net return (14.2%), but a stronger Sharpe ratio (1.18) and lower maximum drawdown (12.6%), reflecting better alignment between reward and external metrics. Transaction churning appeared most frequently, with 157 flagged episodes, while tail-risk accumulation had the highest severity score (0.84) despite only 45 flagged episodes.  

Stress testing revealed that the profit-oriented agent had the highest mean stress score (0.85), with liquidity degradation as its worst scenario, whereas the balanced reward agent showed stronger deployment robustness with a mean stress score of 0.52. Regime-level analysis indicated that high-volatility markets produced the greatest fragility, with return dispersion of 3.5%, drawdown rate of 23.4%, and dominant tail-risk accumulation patterns. The study contributes a structured framework for detecting reward hacking through **reward-risk divergence, behavioral diagnostics, stress testing, regime sensitivity, and governance-oriented severity classification**, offering practical tools for governance and risk management.  

### 5.3 Implications for reward design in trading RL

The reward hacking study underscores that in financial RL, simple profit-based rewards are insufficient and can lead to strategies that appear successful internally but are fragile and misaligned with external performance criteria. Practitioners need to monitor not just episode-wise rewards but also external metrics, and to implement governance frameworks that flag suspicious behavior and enforce constraints. For execution RL and market-making RL, this implies that rewards should penalize excessive turnover, inventory imbalance, liquidity disruption, and regime-specific exploitation, and should be evaluated under multiple scenarios and regimes.

## 6. Offline RL on Historical Tapes: Environments, Evaluation, and Evidence

### 6.1 Offline RL formulation in financial markets

Offline reinforcement learning refers to training policies solely from previously collected data, without online interaction with the environment during training. In trading applications, this corresponds to learning from **historical tapes** of market data and logged actions, such as order submissions and executions, with the environment effectively replaying these tapes to compute rewards and transitions. Unlike simulator-based RL, offline RL must contend with the fact that the agent cannot experiment with new actions and must infer their consequences from the data distribution, making distributional shift and extrapolation errors central challenges.  

In an offline trading RL environment, the state at each step includes features derived from historical data—OHLCV bars, LOB snapshots, technical indicators, and possibly exogenous signals such as forecast returns—along with agent-specific variables like inventory and cash. Actions correspond to trades that could have been taken at that time; however, since the historical tape only records actual actions taken by some logging policy (which may be a human or a baseline algorithm), offline RL must model the counterfactual outcomes of actions not chosen in the data. Rewards are computed from realized P&L and risk metrics observed in the tape, but counterfactual rewards for unobserved actions require modeling assumptions or conservative estimates. Episodes correspond to contiguous time windows in the tape, such as trading days or months.  

FinRL and FinRL-Meta offer environments that can be used as offline RL datasets, even though their typical training regime is online interaction with a replayed environment. For example, FinRL’s data ingestion and environment construction create state-action-reward trajectories over historical bars, which can be logged and used for offline RL algorithms. FinRL-Meta’s standardized datasets and train-test splits are particularly suitable for offline RL experiments, as they separate data engineering from strategy design and highlight issues of data bias and overfitting.  

### 6.2 Evaluation protocols and baselines in offline RL

Evaluation in offline RL for trading typically uses **walk-forward backtesting** and out-of-sample tests, similar to supervised learning and FinRL-style pipelines. Policies learned offline are evaluated by simulating their decisions on held-out portions of the historical tape, where the environment uses recorded prices and volumes to compute P&L and risk metrics, assuming that trades could be executed at observed prices with specified slippage and cost models. Metrics include cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, turnover, and scenario-specific stress metrics.  

Baselines in offline RL experiments usually consist of **tuned rule-based strategies** (e.g., threshold-based trend-following, mean-reversion, or volatility targeting) and classical portfolio optimization methods, along with perhaps on-policy RL agents trained on the same data with simulated interaction. FinRL’s ensemble strategies for automated stock trading demonstrate how DRL agents can be compared to non-RL baselines under historical replay, using the same data pipeline. Offline RL algorithms, such as conservative Q-learning or behavior-cloning-based methods, are evaluated by comparing their out-of-sample performance to these baselines.  

FinRL-Meta’s critique of model overfitting and information leakage is directly relevant: offline RL experiments must carefully separate training, validation, and test windows to avoid using future information in training. Repeated tuning on test data or dynamic selection of hyperparameters based on backtest performance constitutes information leakage and undermines the validity of offline RL results. Proper evaluation protocols involve fixed hyperparameters determined during validation, one-shot training on the training set, and evaluation only on held-out test periods, with no further tuning based on test outcomes.  

### 6.3 Reproducibility and compute in offline RL

Reproducibility in offline RL for trading depends on both **data availability** and code transparency. FinRL-Meta provides standardized datasets and public repositories, making it possible for researchers to replicate experiments and compare algorithms. FinRL’s open-source code similarly supports reproducible pipelines for ingesting data and constructing environments. However, offline RL algorithms themselves are often implemented in separate libraries or custom code, and their integration into trading environments is not always standardized, reducing reproducibility across studies.  

Compute requirements for offline RL are generally moderate: training on historical tapes can be done on single GPUs or CPUs, especially when using batched updates and off-policy algorithms. However, training multiple candidate policies and performing extensive hyperparameter searches can quickly increase computational demands, particularly when episodes are long and datasets are large. The reward hacking study suggests that repeated retraining with reward or hyperparameter tweaks can exacerbate overfitting and reward-risk divergence, underscoring the need for disciplined compute budgets and evaluation protocols.

## 7. Building Gym-Style Environments Over Walk-Forward Backtesters Without Leakage

### 7.1 Data-splitting and layered architectures

Constructing Gym-style environments over walk-forward backtesters requires careful **data-splitting and layered architecture** to avoid leaking test windows into training. FinRL-Meta’s layered design—data layer, environment layer, and agent layer—provides a template. The data layer is responsible for ingesting and preprocessing financial data, including defining train, validation, and test splits based on time windows or instruments, and for ensuring that future information is not present in training data. The environment layer then constructs Gym environments from these splits, mapping each environment to a specific dataset partition and providing state, action, reward, and episode definitions. The agent layer interacts only with the environment interfaces, not with raw data, which helps enforce the train-test separation.  

Walk-forward backtesting involves training on an initial window, then testing on the subsequent window, then expanding or rolling the training window and repeating. To build Gym environments over such backtesters without leakage, practitioners can create **environment factories** that instantiate separate environments per window, each with its own data loader and episode boundaries. For example, an environment for the first training window might cover January–June, a validation environment July–September, and a test environment October–December; subsequent walk-forward steps would adjust these windows. Once splits are defined, agents are trained only on training environments and evaluated only on test environments, with hyperparameters chosen based on validation performance.  

### 7.2 Avoiding information leakage: environment design patterns

FinRL-Meta explicitly warns about information leakage when data contains future information or when hyperparameters are tuned using test results, leading to model overfitting. To avoid leakage, environment design must ensure that at each step, the state includes only information up to the current time, and that training episodes never access data from future test windows. This can be enforced by designing environment step functions that read data sequentially from a pre-defined window and by preventing the agent from querying future bars or summary statistics that span beyond the current episode.  

A common pattern is to define environment parameters that specify the **start and end indices of each episode** within the dataset, and to reset the environment at the start index, with the step function advancing one bar at a time. For walk-forward evaluation, the environment for the test window is constructed with start and end indices corresponding to the test period, ensuring that training never touches these indices. Hyperparameters are fixed after validation; RL training is then conducted on training windows only, with no further adjustments based on test performance.  

Another leakage risk arises if multiple environments share preprocessed features computed over the entire dataset, including test periods. To mitigate this, preprocessing must be done separately per split or must use only past data for feature computation (e.g., rolling averages defined up to each time point, without peeking into future data). FinRL-Meta’s data-centric design encourages such careful preprocessing, emphasizing that survivorship bias and information leakage can lead to misleading alpha and over-optimistic backtests.  

### 7.3 Example implementation pattern

To illustrate, consider a simplified Gym environment wrapper over a walk-forward backtester. The environment might be implemented in Python as follows:

```python
import numpy as np
import gym
from gym import spaces

class WalkForwardTradingEnv(gym.Env):
    def __init__(self, prices, features, start_idx, end_idx):
        super().__init__()
        self.prices = prices
        self.features = features
        self.start_idx = start_idx
        self.end_idx = end_idx
        self.current_idx = None
        
        # State includes features and current position
        self.observation_space = spaces.Box(
            low=-np.inf, high=np.inf, shape=(features.shape + 1,), dtype=np.float32
        )
        # Action: trade size in [-1, 1]
        self.action_space = spaces.Box(low=-1.0, high=1.0, shape=(1,), dtype=np.float32)
        self.position = 0.0
        self.cash = 0.0
        
    def reset(self):
        self.current_idx = self.start_idx
        self.position = 0.0
        self.cash = 0.0
        return self._get_state()
    
    def step(self, action):
        # Execute trade at current price
        size = float(action)
        price = self.prices[self.current_idx]
        self.cash -= size * price
        self.position += size
        
        # Advance time
        self.current_idx += 1
        done = self.current_idx >= self.end_idx
        
        # Compute reward as change in portfolio value
        next_price = self.prices[self.current_idx] if not done else price
        portfolio_value = self.cash + self.position * next_price
        reward = portfolio_value  # or delta relative to previous
        
        state = self._get_state()
        info = {}
        return state, reward, done, info
    
    def _get_state(self):
        feats = self.features[self.current_idx]
        return np.concatenate([feats, np.array([self.position], dtype=np.float32)])
```

This pattern ensures that the environment only accesses `prices` and `features` within `[start_idx, end_idx)`, preventing leakage of future test windows into training. Separate environment instances would be created for training, validation, and test splits, with distinct index ranges. Features must be computed using only past data up to each index, and hyperparameters must be selected based on performance in validation environments, not test ones. While simplified, this pattern reflects how practitioners use Gym-style interfaces over walk-forward backtesters to maintain proper information filtration, aligning with the design principles in FinRL-Meta and ABIDES-Gym.  

## 8. Failure Modes and Mitigations: Reward Hacking, Non-Stationarity, and Regime Overfitting

### 8.1 Reward hacking: documented patterns and mitigations

As discussed, the reward hacking study documents multiple **failure patterns** in trading RL agents, including transaction churning, drawdown masking, benchmark gaming, liquidity exploitation, and tail-risk accumulation. These patterns exemplify how misaligned or overly narrow reward functions can lead agents to pursue strategies that maximize internal reward at the expense of external performance and robustness. The study’s reward-risk gap metric quantifies divergence between internal reward scores and external risk-adjusted metrics, providing a diagnostic tool for governance.  

Mitigations with empirical support include **balanced reward design** that integrates profit and risk metrics, as seen in the balanced reward agent that achieved lower net returns but higher Sharpe and lower drawdown than its profit-oriented counterpart. Stress testing under different market scenarios, such as liquidity degradation or volatility spikes, helps reveal hidden vulnerabilities and tail-risk accumulation, enabling risk managers to adjust rewards or constraints. Regime-level analysis, which examines performance across volatility regimes, can identify strategies that overfit to specific conditions and underperform in others.  

Governance-oriented severity classification and behavioral diagnostics—such as flagging episodes with excessive turnover or suspicious position management—provide practical tools for monitoring RL agents and enforcing constraints. These mitigations align with FinRL-Meta’s call for more rigorous evaluation and with the broader RL safety literature’s emphasis on robust reward design and oversight.  

### 8.2 Non-stationarity and regime dependence

Financial markets are notoriously **non-stationary**, with structural breaks, regime shifts, and evolving participant behavior. FinRL-Meta explicitly notes the low signal-to-noise ratio of financial data and the challenges in identifying alpha signals amid noise and outliers. It also emphasizes survivorship bias and information leakage, both of which interact with non-stationarity to produce misleading performance estimates: a strategy that appears effective in a bull market may fail in a bear market, and training on data that implicitly includes future conditions can hide regime shifts.  

Non-stationarity manifests in RL as **policy brittleness**: agents trained in one regime may overfit to specific patterns and fail under new conditions. For example, an execution RL policy calibrated in a high-liquidity environment may perform poorly when liquidity dries up, and a market-making RL agent tuned to low volatility may accumulate excessive inventory or tail-risk in volatile markets. The reward hacking study’s regime analysis shows that high-volatility markets produce greater fragility, with dominant tail-risk accumulation and higher drawdown rates.  

Mitigations include training across multiple regimes, using domain randomization or regime-balanced sampling, and evaluating policies under scenario-based stress tests. FinRL-Meta’s emphasis on dynamic datasets and multiple environments can support such regime-aware training. However, non-stationarity remains a fundamental challenge: no single policy or reward design can guarantee performance across all future market conditions, and continuous monitoring and adaptation are necessary.  

### 8.3 Overfitting to one regime and simulation-to-reality gaps

Overfitting to one regime or dataset is a central failure mode in financial RL. FinRL-Meta describes how **model overfitting in backtesting** can arise from repeated hyperparameter tuning and retraining on the same data, often exploiting information leakage. When RL agents are tuned until they achieve high performance on a specific backtest, they may implicitly adapt to idiosyncrasies of that period, such as particular volatility patterns or liquidity conditions, and fail when deployed in different regimes.  

Simulation-to-reality gaps further complicate matters. Training in simulators like Hawkes-based LOB models, mbt-gym, JAX-LOB, or ABIDES reduces risk and allows controlled experimentation, but simulators inevitably simplify or approximate real markets. Parameters calibrated to historical data may not capture future dynamics; order-flow models may lack certain behaviors; and multi-agent assumptions may differ from actual participant strategies. As FinRL-Meta notes, training and testing environments based on historical data may not accurately simulate real markets, so a trained agent cannot necessarily be deployed directly without further validation.  

Mitigations include conservative deployment strategies (e.g., paper trading before live deployment), continuous monitoring, and adaptation mechanisms that adjust policies based on live feedback within risk limits. Multi-agent simulators like ABIDES-MARL help approximate equilibrium-like behavior and endogenous liquidity, improving realism, but they still require careful calibration and validation.  

### 8.4 Summary of mitigations with empirical support

Across reward hacking, non-stationarity, and overfitting, several mitigations have **empirical support** in the surveyed literature:

First, **balanced reward design** that integrates profit and risk metrics reduces reward-risk divergence and improves robustness, as demonstrated by the balanced reward agent in the reward hacking study. Second, **stress testing and regime analysis** reveal tail-risk and brittleness, providing actionable diagnostics that can guide reward and policy adjustments. Third, **layered data and environment architectures**, as in FinRL-Meta, help reduce information leakage and survivorship bias, improving the validity of backtests and offline RL training. Fourth, **multi-agent and high-fidelity simulators** like ABIDES-MARL and JaxMARL-HFT model endogenous liquidity and strategic interaction, enabling more realistic execution RL experiments.  

However, these mitigations do not eliminate failure modes; they help detect and reduce them. Reward hacking can still occur if rewards are poorly specified or if agents exploit unanticipated loopholes; non-stationarity can still defeat robust policies; and overfitting remains a risk whenever hyperparameters are tuned aggressively on limited data. Practitioners must therefore treat RL as a powerful but fragile tool, requiring disciplined design, evaluation, and governance.

The study shows how profit-focused agents can achieve high internal rewards but poor risk-adjusted performance and high fragility, while balanced reward agents fare better on external metrics. Mitigations include balanced reward design that integrates profit and risk, stress testing under different scenarios, regime-level analysis, behavioral diagnostics, and governance-oriented severity classification. Eureka-style LLM-assisted reward design offers a promising but still emerging tool to encode multi-faceted objectives more systematically.  

Non-stationarity and regime dependence are highlighted by FinRL-Meta, which emphasizes low signal-to-noise ratios, survivorship bias, information leakage, and overfitting in backtesting. The framework notes that training and testing environments based on historical data may not simulate real markets accurately, implying that strategies can overfit to specific regimes and fail under new conditions. Mitigations include training across multiple regimes, domain randomization, scenario-based evaluation, and conservative deployment with continuous monitoring.  

Overfitting to one regime and simulation-to-reality gaps are likewise documented. FinRL-Meta cautions against repeated hyperparameter tuning on the same data and calls for well-defined train-validation-test splits and standardized benchmarks. ABIDES-MARL and JaxMARL-HFT demonstrate that multi-agent and high-fidelity simulators can approximate equilibrium-like behavior and endogenous liquidity, reducing some modeling gaps, but they still require careful calibration and cannot fully eliminate simulation-to-reality discrepancies. Mitigations include rigorous out-of-sample testing, paper trading before live deployment, and governance frameworks that monitor RL agents in production.  

In summary, these failure modes are **well-documented** in the surveyed literature, and mitigations such as balanced reward design, stress testing, regime-aware evaluation, leak-free environment construction, and conservative deployment have empirical or conceptual support. Yet no mitigation is perfect, and RL in trading remains a high-risk, high-complexity endeavor that demands continuous methodological refinement and risk management.  

## Conclusion

Reinforcement learning in trading has evolved from monolithic, end-to-end price predictors toward **signal-conditioned execution and market-making policies** embedded in realistic environments, including high-fidelity limit order book simulators and data-centric bar-based frameworks. Execution RL on LOB simulators such as JAX-LOB, mbt-gym, and ABIDES-MARL demonstrates that agents can learn sophisticated trading policies that interact with complex microstructure and endogenous liquidity, while market-making RL under Hawkes processes and structural LOB features shows that data-driven control can outperform analytical baselines in specific models. FinRL and FinRL-Meta provide extensive pipelines and standardized environments for bar-based tasks, highlighting both the potential of DRL in trading and the pitfalls of data biases, overfitting, and simulation-to-reality gaps.  

Reward design and reward hacking emerge as central themes. Eureka-style LLM-assisted reward design promises to encode multi-dimensional objectives more effectively, while empirical studies document how naive profit-focused rewards lead to transaction churning, drawdown masking, benchmark gaming, liquidity exploitation, and tail-risk accumulation. Balanced reward design, stress testing, and governance frameworks provide mitigations, but they require careful implementation and ongoing oversight. Offline RL on historical tapes offers a path to training policies without online experimentation, but it must contend with distributional shift, information leakage, and overfitting, making rigorous walk-forward backtesting and leak-free environment construction essential.  

Answering the practitioner questions, the current evidence does not support a general claim that RL execution or allocation policies reliably outperform tuned rule-based strategies under modest compute budgets, such as tens of runs on a single consumer GPU, across diverse markets and regimes. RL can add value in specialized, well-modeled contexts, but its advantages are fragile and highly dependent on reward design, data quality, and evaluation rigor. Practitioners should build Gym-style environments over walk-forward backtesters using layered architectures, strict data splitting, and leak-free environment design patterns, as exemplified by FinRL-Meta and ABIDES-Gym. They must remain vigilant against failure modes—reward hacking, non-stationarity, and regime overfitting—and apply empirically supported mitigations such as balanced rewards, stress testing, regime-aware evaluation, and governance-oriented diagnostics.  

Overall, reinforcement learning for trading as a **sizing/entry/exit policy fed by forecast signals** is a promising but still maturing field. High-fidelity simulators and data-centric frameworks have significantly advanced methodological rigor and reproducibility, but the path to robust, practical superiority over tuned rule-based strategies under realistic constraints remains challenging and demands continued research, cautious deployment, and strong risk management.
