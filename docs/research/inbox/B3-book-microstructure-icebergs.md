# Order-Book Microstructure, Iceberg Detection, And Order-Flow Forecasting For B3 WIN/WDO Futures

The Brazilian futures market for index (WIN) and dollar (WDO) contracts offers a rich laboratory for microstructure research, because B3’s electronic limit order book exposes granular event-level data and, in some configurations, broker and participant identifiers on trades. Against this backdrop, several strands of international research become directly applicable: iceberg-order detection on CME and GLOBEX feeds, microprice and order-book imbalance as short-horizon predictive signals, toxicity metrics such as VPIN constructed from bulk volume classification, and trade classification techniques including Lee–Ready and tick rules. In parallel, Brazilian retail platforms (especially those built by Nelogica) have popularized broker-level “player tracking,” aggressor-based flow analysis, and claimed iceberg detection, although their implementations are mostly undocumented and proprietary. This report synthesizes these literatures and practices for a B3-specific context, focusing on what can be done with historical order-book events and trade tapes enriched by broker codes. It concludes that three signals are most likely to deliver incremental forecast skill at a cost of approximately two ticks: a high‑resolution microprice built from multi-level depth imbalances; properly classified order-flow imbalance at the trade level; and broker-tagged aggressor imbalance for a small set of persistently informed or dominant participants. Each of these signals requires specific data structures—event-level limit, cancel, and trade messages, participant identifiers, and at least top-of-book snapshots—and each has documented microstructure foundations that distinguish robust, reproducible signals from marketing claims.

## 1. Market Microstructure And B3 Data Context

### 1.1 Limit Order Books, Spread, And Midprice

Modern electronic markets for futures, including WIN and WDO on B3, are structured around a centralized limit order book that continuously matchesincoming buy and sell orders according to price–time priority. In such a book, the best bid is the highest price at which any participant is willing to buy, while the best ask (or offer) is the lowest price at which any participant is willing to sell, each associated with visible queue sizes representing outstanding limit volumes. The bid–ask spread, defined formally as \(S = P_a - P_b\), represents the cost of immediacy: a trader who wishes to trade immediately must cross the spread, paying the ask to buy or hitting the bid to sell, while a passive liquidity provider earns the spread in expectation for bearing inventory risk. The midprice, \(M = (P_a + P_b) / 2\), provides a convenient theoretical benchmark for the asset’s fair value, even though it is not directly executable; in a properly functioning market the spread is non-negative and the mid lies strictly between bid and ask.

This simple midprice abstraction conceals richer structural features of liquidity that are crucial for high-frequency forecasting. The visible limit order book only displays resting limit orders; hidden liquidity in the form of iceberg orders and stop orders remains invisible until triggered, so that the realizable volume at a price may differ substantially from the displayed volume. Furthermore, the depth of the book at and around the midprice—how many contracts are resting at the best bid and ask and at deeper levels—encodes information about order-book imbalance, which research has shown predicts short-term price movements more reliably than the midprice alone. These properties motivate microstructure-derived signals such as microprice and order-flow imbalance, which explicitly incorporate depth and flow information to refine fair-value estimates and directional forecasts.

### 1.2 Order-Book Events Versus L2 Snapshots

For empirical work on B3 futures, it is essential to distinguish between the full event stream and L2 (depth-of-book) snapshots. An event stream comprises individual messages representing limit order submissions, cancellations, modifications, and trade executions; each event modifies the state of the order book, and for markets like CME and GLOBEX, exchange-level order identifiers remain consistent across the lifetime of an order. In such feeds, iceberg orders appear as sequences of new, trade, update, and delete actions tied to a single order ID, often with trades whose reported volumes exceed the currently displayed quantity, because execution can draw on hidden tranches. In contrast, L2 snapshots record the state of the book at discrete time points—typically the aggregated volume at each price level on both sides—without preserving the identity of individual orders or the precise sequence of events that led to the snapshot.

The difference is not merely academic. Event-level data enable reconstruction of the full history of each limit order, including its initial size, any modifications, partial executions, and ultimate cancellation or full execution. This makes certain detection tasks, such as identifying native iceberg orders (those managed by the exchange), virtually mechanical, because they exploit invariants such as persistent order IDs and trade sizes that exceed resting volume. Conversely, with only L2 snapshots and no order identifiers, one cannot easily trace whether a particular refill in the queue belongs to the same iceberg or to a new order from a different participant, forcing the use of heuristic patterns based on volumes and timing that reduce precision. For a B3 practitioner who “has (or will buy) historical order-book events and a trade tape for B3 futures WIN and WDO,” the methodological frontier is therefore broader than for someone restricted to L2 snapshots, because event-level microstructure methods from CME, GLOBEX, and equity markets become transferable in principle.

### 1.3 B3 Connectivity And Participant Codes

The B3 exchange provides connectivity via FIX sessions whose identities are managed through CompID fields, which identify the counterparty at the session level but do not directly encode operator or brokerage information. Within these sessions, however, order and participant identifiers are embedded in FIX messages in standardized ways that make participant-level analysis possible when such fields are present in the historical data. For example, ClOrdID is a unique client order identifier string of up to 35 characters that, in B3’s specification, begins with the participant’s code in the first eight positions, padded with leading zeros if necessary, followed by date components and a four-character generator identifier that can distinguish instances of client systems. Messages sent by the exchange itself use prefixes such as “B3” plus a system identifier, also padded with zeros as per specification. Another field encodes the participant’s numerical code directly, functioning as a stable participant identifier across messages; for messages sent by B3, this is the ID of the participant who requested the message, while for messages received by B3 it is the ID of the partner participant.

These FIX conventions imply that, when full FIX feeds or properly decoded order-book event data are available, each order and each trade can be linked to a specific B3 participant or brokerage, enabling “broker-tagged flow” analyses. Brazilian retail platforms that claim to monitor “players” or broker activity typically ingest this participant-level information from B3’s iMERCADO or other feeds and aggregate trades and positions at the level of brokers or algorithmic agents. For a researcher or trader, access to participant codes extends the standard microstructure toolkit by allowing one to compute order-flow imbalance, aggressor activity, and inventory changes not only at the aggregate level but broken down by participant categories, which opens the door to “follow the informed trader” strategies, herding detection, and cross-sectional analysis of broker behavior.

## 2. Iceberg-Order Detection Methods And Their Data Requirements

### 2.1 Definitions And Microstructure Importance Of Iceberg Orders

An iceberg order is a special type of limit order in which only a fraction of the total order size—the “peak” or displayed quantity—is visible in the limit order book at any given time, with the remainder of the volume hidden. When the peak is fully executed, the exchange automatically refills the order by displaying another tranche of the hidden volume, and this process repeats until the entire initial order is traded or cancelled. GLOBEX, for example, supports iceberg orders directly: in addition to price, side, and total size, the participant must specify a “max show” parameter that caps the size of the visible fraction, leaving the rest as hidden volume. Practically, these orders enable large traders to reduce market impact by splitting their execution across multiple small peaks, while retaining the guarantee that the hidden volume is resting at the same price and will be replenished automatically when execution depletes the visible queue.

From the perspective of liquidity measurement and high-frequency trading, iceberg orders, unbroadcast orders, and phantom orders all contribute to a discrepancy between displayed and realizable volume. The visible LOB may understate the true supply or demand at a price when hidden volume is present, or may overstate it when phantom orders are cancelled before they can be executed. Because order-book volume is a key determinant of short-term price impact and the ability of market makers to absorb order flow without re-pricing quotes, the presence of icebergs has first-order implications for microprice estimation, order-flow imbalance measurement, and risk management. Detecting iceberg orders ex post in historical data allows a researcher to reconstruct the true liquidity state and study the interaction between hidden and visible volume; detecting them in real time enables market makers to adjust spreads and trading strategies to avoid being “picked off” by large hidden orders that might suddenly consume their quotes.

Iceberg orders can be categorized into native icebergs, which are managed by the exchange and have explicit message-level semantics, and synthetic icebergs, which are implemented by market participants through sequences of ordinary limit orders that mimic iceberg behavior.\ Native icebergs are easier to detect mechanically because exchanges such as CME or GLOBEX maintain the same order ID throughout the iceberg’s life and often report trade sizes that exceed the current displayed volume, signaling the presence of hidden tranches. Synthetic icebergs, in contrast, are processed identically to non-iceberg orders by the exchange, so they must be inferred from patterns in arriving orders and trades that are consistent with iceberg-like refill behavior, such as repeated small peak orders at the same price that replenish immediately after execution.\ These distinctions map directly to different detection methods and data requirements, which are crucial to evaluate for a B3 setting where the presence and encoding of native iceberg orders may differ from CME and GLOBEX.

### 2.2 Native Iceberg Detection On CME: Event-Stream Methods

A recent study on CME data, “CME Iceberg Order Detection and Prediction,” proposes a method for detecting native and synthetic iceberg orders using full event-stream data and trade summary messages. In CME’s native iceberg implementation, the exchange manages replenishment internally, so the displayed quantity is refreshed via update actions on the same order ID when tranches are consumed. Crucially, trades against these orders may be larger than the currently resting display quantity, because execution can draw on both visible and hidden volume; CME’s trade summary messages report the full executed volume, including the hidden part. This pair of properties—persistent order IDs across refreshes and occasional trades with volumes exceeding displayed quantities—provides a robust basis for detection. Native iceberg orders enter the book as limit orders which may or may not be traded upon arrival; after the visible volume is fully traded, the next tranche appears through a modification of the same order, preserving its ID.

The CME detection logic can therefore be described as recognition of specific new–trade–update–delete patterns in the event stream. The algorithm monitors limit orders and associated trades, marking an order as ordinary unless two conditions occur: a trade is observed with size strictly greater than the resting display quantity, or the order is fully traded (resting volume goes to zero) and subsequently modified to have non-zero volume again. Once either condition is met, the order is labeled as an iceberg, and the algorithm continues to track its trade–modify cycle until it is completely executed or cancelled, at which point the order is deleted from the book. Because the order ID never changes and trade summary messages include actual trade volumes, this procedure yields unambiguous and accurate detection of native icebergs, in the authors’ words, without requiring any probabilistic inference. The data requirement is a full event stream with limit, trade, and modify messages, including resting display quantities and trade summary volumes; L2 snapshots alone are insufficient, because they lack order identities and may not expose trade sizes relative to resting volume.

In addition to detection, the CME study uses the identified iceberg orders to train a prediction model for remaining hidden volume based on the Kaplan–Meier estimator, treating partial executions and cancellations as censored observations. By modeling the survival distribution of iceberg volume conditional on observed executions, the authors estimate the probability that additional hidden tranches remain after a given amount of visible volume has been consumed, which allows them to forecast residual iceberg size in real time. While precise numerical measures of precision or recall are not highlighted in the excerpt, the authors emphasize the conceptual ease and accuracy of native iceberg detection under CME’s message semantics, suggesting that classification errors are negligible when the event stream is complete and correctly parsed. The paper does not advertise publicly released code in the abstract or excerpts, implying that replication relies on implementing the described rules against proprietary CME feeds rather than downloading an off-the-shelf library, though the detection logic is straightforward enough for an experienced market data engineer to code from scratch.

### 2.3 Synthetic Iceberg Detection: Heuristic Event-Stream Patterns

Detecting synthetic iceberg orders is more challenging because the exchange does not treat them differently from ordinary limit orders; all processing is identical, and there are no message-level flags indicating hidden volume. Synthetic icebergs are managed by market participants themselves, typically via client-side execution algorithms that submit a sequence of small limit orders at the same price, refilling the queue as visible tranches are executed, in order to mimic the behavior of a native iceberg without relying on exchange support. Since the exchange does not preserve a single order ID across these tranches, the only way to infer synthetic icebergs is to look for patterns in the order stream consistent with refill behavior: for instance, when a limit order is fully executed and, within a short time interval \(dt\), a new limit order with similar attributes (price, side) and volume appears at the same price level.

The CME paper approaches synthetic iceberg detection by observing limit orders arriving within a short time frame after trades, under a set of assumptions about participant behavior. The algorithm identifies candidate iceberg events when it sees an order executed and, within \(dt\), a new order of the same side at the same price with similar or identical size; by stitching together these sequences of trade and refill events, it constructs synthetic iceberg trajectories. A synthetic iceberg is considered complete if a tranche (visible order) is executed and no refill orders follow within \(dt\), at which point the algorithm assumes that the trader’s hidden interest at that price has been exhausted. Unlike native iceberg detection, this method is heuristic and cannot guarantee correctness, because it does not know whether the refill order was submitted by the same participant or represents a coincidental new order from another trader. Its precision depends on market liquidity, the choice of \(dt\), and behavioral assumptions such as the likelihood that multiple traders independently choose identical prices and sizes in rapid succession.

Synthetic iceberg detection thus requires a full event stream with timestamped limit and trade messages but does not benefit from stable order IDs; L2 snapshots cannot distinguish whether replenishment arises from a single trader or multiple traders. Precision and recall of synthetic detection are inherently empirical and instrument-specific, and the CME article frames these methods as heuristic and assumption-dependent rather than as unambiguous classification rules. As with native icebergs, the paper uses detected synthetic icebergs to train survival-based models of hidden volume, but the underlying detection uncertainty implies that predictive estimates of synthetic iceberg size are noisier than for native icebergs. There is no indication of public code for synthetic detection in the excerpts, so users must implement the heuristic pattern recognition themselves, tailoring parameters such as \(dt\) and similarity thresholds to B3 futures’ liquidity conditions and participant behavior.

### 2.4 GLOBEX Hidden Liquidity And Christensen–Woodmansey Algorithm

A seminal contribution for futures markets is the article “Prediction of Hidden Liquidity in the Limit Order Book of GLOBEX Futures” by Hugh L. Christensen and Robert Woodmansey, published in the Journal of Trading in 2013. Their motivation is to present an online algorithm that can detect iceberg orders and predict their hidden volume for major derivatives exchanges, focusing on the e-mini S&P 500 future (ES) on GLOBEX. GLOBEX supports iceberg orders natively through a “max show” parameter that caps visible volume and hides the remainder, similar to CME’s implementation, but Christensen and Woodmansey emphasize that the limit order book may fail to display the true liquidity state for three reasons: iceberg orders, unbroadcast orders, and phantom orders. Unbroadcast orders arise from implied pricing mechanisms in certain products, while phantom orders are those that appear in the book but are cancelled before execution.

Christensen and Woodmansey categorize iceberg detection methods into three broad classes: active algorithms, model-based algorithms, and frequentist algorithms. Active algorithms attempt to detect icebergs by “pinging” the order book with small test orders that the participant never intends to have filled, observing whether the depth at a price refills after a probe execution—it is a form of experimental microstructure, but it requires live trading and cannot be applied to historical data retroactively. Model-based algorithms rely on parametric models of the order book and price impact, inferring hidden liquidity from deviations between expected and realized volumes or price changes; they can be applied to historical data but depend heavily on model assumptions. Frequentist algorithms, which include their main contribution, use empirical patterns in limit order book data—such as the frequency and magnitude of refills relative to executions—to detect iceberg-hidden volume without imposing strong parametric structure.

Their GLOBEX-specific algorithm sits on the exchange feed handler and is implementable with millisecond latency, meaning it consumes event-stream data in real time, processes it with low computational overhead, and generates iceberg detection outputs fast enough for trading applications. By modeling market microstructure using limit order book data, the algorithm detects iceberg orders and predicts remaining hidden volume, allowing market makers to “pick off” iceberg orders by adjusting their quotes to anticipate large hidden execution. When applied to ES data from 2011, they estimate that approximately 9% of the volume in the limit order book is composed of iceberg orders, indicating a substantial hidden liquidity component in a highly liquid futures market. The authors highlight that their algorithm can be applied to explicit sub-books that do not support implied orders and, in parallel with an unbroadcast algorithm, to implied books that require separate treatment.

In terms of data, the Christensen–Woodmansey algorithm relies on event-level LOB messages with order IDs, prices, sizes, and execution flags, similar to CME’s footing. The detection rules, while not fully detailed in the excerpt, likely exploit sequences in which realized volume at a price level exceeds previously displayed volume or where orders are fully executed but depth replenishes in patterns consistent with iceberg behavior, aligning conceptually with CME’s simpler native detection. Precision is assessed through simulation on historical data, with the 9% estimate reflecting the proportion of volume associated with detected icebergs; their low-latency design suggests that real-time detection is feasible, although they do not explicitly claim perfect classification. As with CME, the Journal of Trading article focuses on algorithmic description and performance rather than code release; practitioners must implement the algorithms themselves based on the paper’s specification, and public open-source code is not advertised in the journal context.

### 2.5 Snapshot-Based Detection And Its Limitations

For markets or data providers that only offer L2 snapshots instead of full event streams, iceberg detection becomes much more speculative. Without order IDs and precise trade–modify sequences, a researcher can only observe aggregated volumes at each price level at discrete times and the net trades that occur between snapshots through the trade tape. Hidden liquidity could be inferred when, for example, a large number of contracts is executed at a price level across several trades, yet the visible depth in snapshots does not deplete as much as expected or refills quickly, suggesting that some source of replenishment is present beyond ordinary limit flow. However, this pattern could equally well be generated by multiple independent traders submitting new limit orders, by algorithmic market makers replenishing their quotes, or by synthetic icebergs implemented by one or more participants, making attribution to a particular iceberg ambiguous.

Frequentist snapshot methods might model expected visible depth trajectories conditional on observed order-flow and treat deviations as signals of hidden liquidity, but precision would be intrinsically lower than in event-stream methods because the mapping from hidden to visible behavior is many-to-one. The literature acknowledges that iceberg detection with only snapshots falls into model-based or heuristic categories, necessarily relying on assumptions about participant behavior and book dynamics. For a B3 researcher with full event-stream data, it is therefore recommended to avoid snapshot-only detection for iceberg orders and instead replicate event-based algorithms akin to CME and GLOBEX methods, preserving order IDs where available and aligning trade sizes with resting volumes. If B3 does not expose explicit iceberg semantics, synthetic detection heuristics similar to CME’s approach may still be valuable, but they will carry the same caveats about precision and require instrument-specific calibration of timing windows and similarity thresholds.

### 2.6 Comparative Summary Of Iceberg Detection Methods

To synthesize the discussion, it is helpful to compare iceberg detection methods along four dimensions: data required (full event stream versus L2 snapshots), detection rule structure (mechanical versus heuristic), reported precision or reliability, and public code availability. CME native iceberg detection and GLOBEX iceberg algorithms fall in the category of event-stream, mechanical rules with high conceptual precision, because exchange-managed iceberg semantics guarantee stable order IDs and trade volume reporting that map cleanly to hidden volume detection. Synthetic iceberg detection on CME uses event-stream data but applies heuristic rules based on temporal proximity and price–size similarity of refills, leading to instrument-dependent precision that requires empirical evaluation. Snapshot-based detection and active pinging methods rely more heavily on modeling and experimental design, with lower reproducibility and precision in historical data environments. Across these methods, open-source code is rarely provided in academic publications, so practitioners must implement algorithms manually; however, the mechanical nature of native iceberg detection makes coding feasible, while synthetic and snapshot methods demand greater expertise and calibration.

For B3 WIN and WDO futures, the practical route depends on whether B3 supports native iceberg orders with clear FIX semantics and whether those semantics are exposed in the historical data purchased. If native iceberg support is available and order IDs or hidden-volume flags can be recovered from FIX fields, CME-like detection can be implemented with high reliability, relying on trade sizes exceeding resting display quantities and order modifications that refill visible peaks. If only ordinary limit orders are present but participants implement synthetic icebergs, heuristics analogous to CME’s synthetic detection can be applied to event-stream data, with explicit recognition of their assumption dependence. In the absence of detailed order IDs, snapshot-based frequentist methods may serve as exploratory tools, but their signals should be treated as noisy approximations of hidden liquidity rather than precise classifiers, particularly when designing trading strategies whose profitability depends on avoiding large hidden orders.

## 3. Order-Flow Signals And Predictive Evidence At 1–5 Minute Horizons

### 3.1 Static Order-Book Imbalance, Microprice, And Short-Horizon Forecasting

Static order-book imbalance measures the relative depth on the bid and ask sides of the book, typically at the best price levels, and has been widely studied as a predictor of short-term price movements. In its simplest form, order-book imbalance at the top of the book is defined as the ratio \(I = V_b / (V_b + V_a)\), where \(V_b\) and \(V_a\) are the volumes at the best bid and best ask, respectively. When the book is balanced, \(I \approx 0.5\); when the bid side is heavier, \(I\) approaches 1; and when the ask side dominates, \(I\) approaches 0. Empirically, a book heavy on the bid signals incipient buying pressure, making upward price moves more likely, whereas a heavy ask side suggests downward pressure, making downward moves more likely. These tendencies persist over short horizons—seconds to a few minutes—before new orders and trades wash out the imbalance, and static imbalance serves as a “snapshot” signal aligned with the immediate microstructure state.

The microprice concept refines this intuition by constructing a depth-weighted estimate of fair value that leans toward the side with more pressure. Stoikov’s microprice is originally defined as the expected future midprice, conditioned on the current state of the order book—specifically, the processes of midprice, imbalance, and spread—pushed far enough ahead that the current imbalance has dissipated. Formally, if one considers the sequence of times \(\tau_i\) when the midprice changes, the microprice at time \(t\) can be written as \(P^{\text{micro}}_t = \lim_{i \to \infty} E[M_{\tau_i} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\), where \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is the sigma-field generated by the mid, imbalance, and spread processes up to time \(t\). Under assumptions that midprice increments are independent of the midprice level and that the order book state can be summarized by imbalance \(I_t\) and spread \(S_t\), Stoikov shows that the conditional expectation can be expressed as \(P^{\text{micro}}_t = M_t + \sum_{k=1}^i g_k(I_t, S_t)\), and in the limit, as a single adjustment function \(g(I_t, S_t)\) added to the current midprice.

Practical implementations often approximate microprice using only top-of-book data via a weighted average formula. One standard definition is \(P_{\text{micro}} = \frac{P_b V_a + P_a V_b}{V_b + V_a}\), which can be rearranged to \(P_{\text{micro}} = I P_a + (1 - I) P_b\), highlighting its dependence on imbalance. When \(V_b\) exceeds \(V_a\), so that \(I > 0.5\), the microprice lies closer to the ask price than to the bid, anticipating an upward move; conversely, when \(V_a\) dominates, the microprice moves closer to the bid, anticipating a downward move. Stoikov refines this linear weighting by fitting an S‑shaped function \(G(I)\) that is flat near \(I = 0.5\) (reflecting minimal fair-value displacement in balanced books) and steep near the extremes (reflecting strong pressure when one side dominates), improving empirical predictive performance. The HFT Book characterizes microprice as “the mid plus a correction for which way the book is leaning” and notes that it is a better one-step-ahead predictor of the next mid-move than the simple mid, making it “the money-ladder’s bottom rung” in order-flow market-making.

Importantly, the microprice construction lends itself to a martingale-like property. Once price is at the microprice, conditional on the current imbalance and spread, the expected next change in midprice is approximately zero, because all predictable drift arising from the static imbalance has been priced in. This property allows microprice to serve both as a fair-value estimator and as a baseline for measuring directional edge: if the actual midprice lies significantly below the microprice, a trader might take long positions expecting reversion; if the mid lies above, short positions might be favorable, within the constraints of transaction costs and market impact. Empirical studies and practitioner experience suggest that microprice-based signals are most effective at short horizons—on the order of seconds to a few minutes—where the static imbalance retains predictive power before new order-book events reset the state, aligning with the user’s interest in 1–5 minute horizons.

### 3.2 High-Resolution Microprice Models Incorporating Deeper Depth

While early microprice formulations focused on top-of-book data, recent work has extended the concept to incorporate higher-order depth information across multiple price ranks. A 2024 preprint on “High-resolution microprice estimates from limit order books” proposes an error-correcting model that augments the classical microprice with information from deeper price levels and recent dynamics of higher-rank imbalances. The authors argue that accurate and fast microprice algorithms should reflect not only best bid and ask imbalance but also supply and demand at other price ranks, because deeper liquidity can absorb or amplify order flow and thereby affect future prices. Their model starts from a current microprice estimate given the spread and best bid–ask imbalance and then adjusts that estimate based on changes in imbalances at deeper levels, effectively building an error-correcting term that captures deviations from what top-of-book data alone would predict.

The computational framework employs hyperdimensional vector Tsetlin machines, a recently proposed symbolic learning architecture that can process high-dimensional categorical data efficiently. Using this framework, the authors construct a fast estimator that, given a vector representation of order-book imbalances across multiple levels, outputs an updated microprice estimate with improved robustness relative to simple midprice or weighted midprice methods. Empirically, they demonstrate that this high-resolution microprice provides a more robust estimate of future prices in the order book, performing better than conventional microprice and midprice in short-horizon prediction tasks. Formally, they continue to write microprice as \(P_{\text{micro}} = M + g(I, S)\), but now \(I\) and \(S\) are extended state variables that include deeper-level imbalances and spread-related features, and \(g\) is learned or calibrated from historical limit order book data.

From a data perspective, high-resolution microprice models require multi-level L2 depth data, not just best bid and ask. The ability to compute aggregate order sizes at several ranks and track their dynamics over time is essential for constructing the hyperdimensional state vector and learning the adjustment function \(g\). For B3 WIN and WDO futures, this implies that an L2 feed with at least, say, the top 5–10 levels on each side is desirable for full implementation, although simpler versions using only the top level remain useful when deeper data are costly or unavailable. Because the authors emphasize computational efficiency and robustness, high-resolution microprice is particularly relevant for algorithmic trading strategies that must process data in real time and adapt to changing depth profiles, making it a prime candidate for short-horizon forecasting at 1–5 minute scales when combined with appropriate event windows and risk controls.

### 3.3 Order-Flow Imbalance, Queueing Models, And Cont’s Framework

Order-flow imbalance (OFI) is a dynamic measure of the net buying versus selling pressure over a time interval, typically constructed as the difference between aggressive buy volume and aggressive sell volume, sometimes normalized by total volume. In contrast to static order-book imbalance, which measures resting volumes at a point in time, OFI captures the flow of orders that increase or decrease queue sizes, including market buys and sells and limit orders that join or leave the book through cancellations. Research in market microstructure has established that OFI predicts short-term price movements: net buy pressure tends to push prices up, while net sell pressure tends to push prices down, with the magnitude of impact depending on liquidity conditions and the degree of imbalance. This predictive power underpins many high-frequency trading strategies that monitor aggressive flows and adjust quotes or inventory accordingly.

Cont’s work on statistical modeling of high-frequency financial data offers a queueing model of limit order book dynamics that clarifies the role of order-flow imbalance. In such models, limit buy orders, limit sell orders, market buy orders, market sell orders, and cancellations are assigned arrival rates, and queue sizes at bid and ask evolve as trades and cancellations deplete queues while new limits replenish them. For most liquid stocks, the rate of arrival of market orders and limit orders is large, but the imbalance between limit orders (which increase queue size) and market orders and cancels (which decrease queue size) is much smaller, often on the order of 1–5% of order flow over a 30-minute interval. Over a time scale \(\tau_1\) much larger than the characteristic times for limit (\(\tau_L\)) and market (\(\tau_M\)) order arrivals, a large number of events \(N \sim \lambda \tau_1\) occur, but the bid–ask imbalance accumulated over the same interval is of order \(\sqrt{N}\), significantly smaller than \(N\), implying that the net drift in queue sizes is modest relative to the gross flow.

This observation supports a modeling stance in which limit order accumulation and disappearance occur roughly at the same rate, with small order-flow imbalances driving price changes through stochastic fluctuations rather than deterministic trends. In practice, OFI over intervals such as 1–5 minutes is computed by aggregating signed trade sizes (using trade classification algorithms) and possibly signed limit and cancel sizes, and then regressing midprice changes on OFI to estimate impact coefficients. The predictive “half-life” of OFI—i.e., the time it takes for its correlation with future price changes to decay by half—is typically short, because new flows quickly dominate old ones, but in liquid futures markets such as ES and, by analogy, WIN/WDO, OFI over 1–5 minute windows remains informative, especially when combined with static microprice signals and depth-based features.

The interplay between OFI and microprice is particularly important. Microprice reads the static imbalance—the sizes resting now—to set fair value, while OFI measures the flow of imbalance (events over a window) to predict the next move. A book heavy on the bid with positive OFI in favor of buys is a stronger bullish signal than either alone, while a heavy bid combined with strong sell OFI may indicate absorption or potential reversal. This suggests that for B3 futures, constructing both static imbalance-based microprice and dynamic OFI signals over 1–5 minute intervals can yield complementary forecasts, with microprice anchoring fair value and OFI capturing incremental directional pressure.

### 3.4 Trade Classification: Lee–Ready, Tick Rules, And Bulk Volume Classification

Accurate OFI computation depends on correctly classifying trades as buyer-initiated or seller-initiated. Two general approaches have been used to infer trade direction: price-based tick tests, which compare the trade price to adjacent trades, and quote-based methods, which compare the trade price to bid and ask prices of the prevailing quote. Lee and Ready’s 1991 paper systematically evaluates these approaches, identifying two potential problems with quote-based methods: quotes may be recorded ahead of trades that triggered them, and trades inside the spread are not readily classifiable. To mitigate these issues, they propose comparing the trade to the quote in effect 5 seconds earlier, rather than the contemporaneous quote, and using tick tests for trades inside the spread. A trade is classified as an uptick if its price is higher than the previous trade, a downtick if lower, and a zero-uptick or zero-downtick if the price is unchanged but the last price change was an uptick or downtick, respectively; upticks and zero-upticks are treated as buys, while downticks and zero-downticks are treated as sells.

Lee and Ready show that misclassifications can be greatly reduced by this hybrid approach and that the tick test, in particular, provides remarkably accurate directional inferences when only price data are available. When full bid–ask quotes are accessible, combining quote-based and tick-based classification yields high accuracy, forming the basis of many transaction-based order imbalance measures. In futures markets where tick-level trade and quote feeds are available, such as CME and B3, using Lee–Ready or closely related algorithms is considered standard for constructing OFI. When only OHLCV bars are available (for example, on platforms without full tick feeds), bulk volume classification (BVC) becomes relevant. Easley, López de Prado, and O’Hara introduce BVC as a method that aggregates trades over short time or volume intervals and uses standardized price changes between the beginning and end of the interval to approximate the percentage of buy and sell order flow.

In the canonical BVC implementation described by Easley et al., each bar’s return is computed as \(\text{return} = ( \text{close} - \text{prior close} ) / \text{prior close}\), and the return is standardized by a rolling estimate of its standard deviation, yielding a z-score. The fraction of buy volume in the bar is then estimated as \(\Phi(z)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, and the fraction of sell volume as \(1 - \Phi(z)\); buy and sell volumes are assigned accordingly and delta is computed as buy volume minus sell volume. The intuition is that bars with strongly positive returns are more likely to have been driven by aggressive buyers, while bars with strongly negative returns are more likely to have been driven by aggressive sellers, with small-return bars estimated to be balanced. On liquid futures with 5‑minute bars, BVC delta correlates with true tick-rule delta at approximately 0.85–0.95, high enough that BVC is usable for many order-flow purposes when true tick-side data are unavailable.

However, more recent work by Carrión and coauthors scrutinizes BVC’s ability to measure informed trading and finds that it underperforms conventional order imbalance measures constructed from true trade signs or Lee–Ready classifications. They argue that evidence supporting BVC largely arises from misspecified tests and that their own tests find no evidence that BVC outperforms OIB_TRUE or OIB_LR in identifying informed trading; indeed, BVC-based order imbalance appears to be significantly less informative. Andersen and Bondarenko, in their critique of VPIN, further show that VPIN metrics constructed using BVC are strongly dependent on volume and volatility innovations and that using more standard transaction-based classification schemes leads to diametrically opposite results relative to Easley et al.’s claims. These findings reinforce the view that, whenever possible, futures researchers should rely on tick-based trade classification methods rather than BVC, using BVC only as a fallback when only OHLCV bars are available.

### 3.5 VPIN, Flow Toxicity, And Predictive Limitations

The volume-synchronized probability of informed trading (VPIN) metric emerges from Easley, López de Prado, and O’Hara’s work on flow toxicity in high-frequency markets. Flow toxicity refers to the risk faced by liquidity providers when trading with traders who have better information than they do; order flow is considered toxic when liquidity providers find themselves selling when they would rather be buying or buying when they would rather be selling. VPIN aims to quantify this toxicity by looking at the imbalance of trade relative to total volume, updated in volume time rather than clock time, making it responsive to high-frequency trading dynamics. The metric requires trades classified as buys or sells, for which the authors develop bulk volume classification as a purportedly more useful method in high-frequency markets than standard classification procedures. VPIN has been touted as a predictor of short-term volatility and as a harbinger of “flash events”—short-term illiquidity crises where market makers suddenly stop trading, resulting in abrupt price drops.

Cornell’s news coverage of this research highlights the claim that flash crashes are now predictable and possibly preventable thanks to VPIN, suggesting that high VPIN levels could alert regulators to flow toxicity early enough to adjust the market and that traders could hedge flash crash risk using VPIN-based strategies. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission and FINRA’s economic advisory board have expressed interest in VPIN, and private firms such as Tudor Investments have reportedly used VPIN research to develop trading algorithms for high-frequency markets. However, subsequent empirical analyses cast doubt on VPIN’s incremental predictive power once standard controls are applied. Andersen and Bondarenko, using tick data for S&P 500 futures, show that VPIN is correlated with trading volume and return volatility innovations by construction, and that whether VPIN is more strongly correlated with volume or volatility depends on implementation details. After controlling for current volume and volatility, they find no evidence of incremental predictive power of VPIN for future volatility and note that VPIN does not attain unusual extremes prior to the flash crash.

Moreover, they show that VPIN’s properties are strongly dependent on the underlying trade classification and that using more standard transaction-based classification schemes leads to behavior opposite to that portrayed in Easley et al.’s work. They argue that VPIN is largely driven—and significantly distorted—by volume and volatility innovations and that transaction-based classification methods are more accurate than bulk volume strategies for constructing VPIN-like metrics. This critique implies that VPIN, while conceptually appealing as a toxicity indicator, may not add much forecasting power beyond simple measures of recent volume and volatility, particularly for short-horizon direction or volatility predictions at 1–5 minute scales. For a B3 futures researcher, VPIN might be considered as a diagnostic tool for extreme conditions but should be treated cautiously as a tradeable signal, especially given its sensitivity to classification choices and the mixed empirical evidence on its efficacy.

### 3.6 Queue-Imbalance Models And Predictive Half-Lives

Queue-imbalance models focus on the dynamics of order queues at bid and ask, modeling how arrivals and departures of orders affect the likelihood of price changes. In Cont’s queueing framework, queue sizes evolve as birth–death processes, with births corresponding to limit orders and deaths corresponding to market orders and cancellations. The probability that the midprice moves up can be linked to the probability that the bid queue depletes before the ask queue, while the probability of a downward move is linked to the opposite event. Static order-book imbalance—as measured by relative queue sizes—thus captures the instantaneous risk of price movement, while dynamic OFI measures the net flow altering queue sizes over time. Queue-imbalance models often yield analytical or numerical expressions for expected waiting times until price changes and for the conditional probabilities of upward versus downward moves, providing microstructure-based forecasts.

The half-life of predictive signals derived from queue-imbalance models depends on how quickly new orders and cancellations alter queue sizes. In highly liquid futures markets, new limits and cancels arrive at high rates, so queues can change dramatically within seconds, reducing the relevance of older imbalance information. Nevertheless, the net imbalance between limit orders and market orders/cancels over intervals such as 1–5 minutes remains small but nonzero, and its statistical effect on price changes, while subtle, is demonstrable in empirical studies. Cont notes that over a 30-minute interval with many events, the accumulated imbalance is of order \(\sqrt{N}\), implying that queue drift is modest but meaningful. For B3 WIN and WDO, which are actively traded, queue-imbalance signals are likely to be most predictive over short horizons, where static imbalances and recent OFI still reflect the current liquidity state before being overwhelmed by new flows. Combining queue-imbalance models with microprice and OFI yields richer forecasts: microprice incorporates imbalance into fair value, OFI measures net pressure, and queueing models offer probabilistic timing of price moves.

## 4. Broker-Tagged Flow: Participant-Level Strategies And Brazilian Context

### 4.1 B3 Participant Codes And Broker-Level Data Architecture

As noted earlier, B3’s FIX specifications define session-level and participant-level identifiers that, when preserved in historical data, enable detailed broker-tagged analyses. CompID fields identify the counterparties in FIX connections but are used only at the session level and do not directly transmit operator or brokerage information beyond the name of the FIX session. In contrast, ClOrdID embeds the participant’s code in the first eight positions of the string, padded with zeros if necessary, followed by date components and a four-position identifier of the message generator, which can be used to distinguish different instances of client systems or algorithms. Additional fields explicitly carry the participant’s numerical identifier, which B3 fills for messages in the iMERCADO catalog based on the participant who requested the message or the partner participant for incoming messages.

These structured identifiers allow platforms and researchers to attribute orders and trades to specific participants or brokers when they parse FIX messages or their downstream encodings in order-book feeds. For example, Brazilian retail platforms such as Profit Ultra integrate these fields to construct tools like Motion Tracker, which monitor the positioning and activities of major participants in relation to specific financial assets. Motion Tracker provides real-time information on broker participation, the net balance of operations (buy minus sell volume) by each player, and the average execution price for each broker, enabling traders to see which brokers are heavily involved at current prices and whether they are net buyers or sellers. By filtering trades to those within the trading session and excluding trades not originating from algorithms, the tool focuses on algorithmic players and orders of interest to active traders. This illustrates how broker-tagged flow can be harnessed to derive participant-level order-flow signals that go beyond aggregate OFI and imbalance.

For a researcher with access to historical order-book events and trade tapes that include buyer and seller broker codes, similar analyses can be performed offline. Trade-level data can be aggregated by broker to compute daily or intraday net aggressive volume (“saldo”), participation rates at specific price levels, and average execution prices, replicating Motion Tracker’s outputs and extending them with statistical tests of persistence and profitability. Order-level data—including limit submissions and cancellations—can be linked to brokers via ClOrdID and participant fields, allowing reconstruction of broker-specific limit order strategies, queue placement behavior, and cancellation patterns. This data richness opens the door to identifying brokers whose flow appears informed or whose strategies systematically profit, enabling “follow the informed trader” approaches grounded in actual performance rather than anecdote.

### 4.2 Participant-Level Flow Persistence And “Follow The Informed Trader”

Academic work on informed trading and flow toxicity emphasizes that order imbalance measures can capture the presence of informed traders whose trades are systematically profitable and adverse to liquidity providers. Easley, López de Prado, and O’Hara argue that flow toxicity arises when liquidity providers are unwittingly trading against better-informed participants, and they propose VPIN as a metric to estimate this toxicity based on volume imbalance and trade intensity. While their focus is on aggregate flow, their conceptual framework suggests that if one can identify subsets of participants whose flows are consistently associated with adverse price moves—i.e., whose net aggressive buying precedes price increases and whose net aggressive selling precedes price decreases—then following those participants could be profitable. Broker-tagged data, such as B3’s participant codes, enable exactly this decomposition: aggregate flows can be broken down by broker, and the predictive content of each broker’s flow can be studied separately.

In a “follow the informed trader” strategy, one might first estimate the predictive relationship between each broker’s aggressor-based OFI and future price changes, controlling for aggregate OFI and other covariates, over a training period. Brokers whose flows exhibit strong positive correlations with subsequent price changes—suggesting that they are consistently informed—could then be tracked in real time, with their net aggressive volumes serving as directional signals. For example, a broker whose net buyside aggressor volume over a 1‑minute interval predicts price increases over the next 1–5 minutes might be followed by taking long positions when that broker accumulates buys and closing or reversing when they switch to net sells. Broker-level flow persistence refers to the tendency of brokers to maintain their trading styles and information advantages over time; if this persistence is high, past performance is informative for future behavior, making broker-tagged signals more reliable.

While the search results do not provide specific empirical studies on broker-level persistence in B3, the presence of tools like Motion Tracker, which prioritize algorithmically active brokers and display their participation and saldo, indicates a belief among practitioners that certain brokers’ flows are more informative than others. Motion Tracker orders players by participation (in Bovespa assets) or saldo (in BM&F assets such as futures), with an option to prioritize active algorithms, suggesting that algorithmic brokers with large net flows are treated as “players” worth following. The tool’s ability to show the average execution price of each broker in real time further supports strategies that monitor whether large brokers are underwater or in profit on their positions, potentially anticipating mean-reversion or trend continuation based on their behavior. These practices can be formalized in statistical frameworks, but their underlying premise—that broker-tagged flow can reveal informed trading—is grounded in microstructure theory on asymmetric information and flow toxicity.

### 4.3 Herding Detection And Cross-Participant Dynamics

Broker-tagged data also enable detection of herding behavior, whereby multiple brokers engage in similar trading patterns, amplifying price moves and liquidity imbalances. Herding can be measured by correlations between brokers’ net OFI vectors over time, by clustering brokers whose flows co-move, or by identifying periods when many brokers simultaneously become net buyers or sellers in the same asset. In the presence of herding, price impact may be larger than predicted by aggregate OFI alone, because the diversity of liquidity providers is reduced; multiple participants are pushing in the same direction, making it more difficult for contrarian liquidity suppliers to stabilize prices. Microstructure theory suggests that herding can arise from information cascades, momentum-chasing strategies, or common reactions to news and volatility shocks.

Although no specific Brazilian academic studies on herding using B3 participant codes are highlighted in the search results, the conceptual tools are available. Motion Tracker’s ability to filter by agents and display only selected brokers’ flows on a chart facilitates visual detection of herding among chosen subsets of players. Traders can observe, for instance, whether several large brokers shift from net selling to net buying around the same time or whether one broker’s aggressive flow appears to lead others’ responses. Combining broker-tagged OFI with microprice, one might study whether herding emerges when microprice signals align strongly with recent flows, reinforcing trends, or whether herding causes overshooting relative to microprice-based fair value, creating mean-reversion opportunities. These analyses move beyond aggregate order-flow metrics into the realm of cross-participant microstructure, leveraging B3’s participant identifier architecture.

### 4.4 Brazilian Retail Practices: “Rastreador de Players”, “Fluxo de Corretoras”, And “Agressão”

Brazilian retail trading culture has developed its own vocabulary and tools around broker-tagged flow and order-book aggression. Terms such as “rastreador de players” (player tracker) and “fluxo de corretoras” (broker flow) refer to tools and strategies aimed at monitoring the actions of major brokers and institutional participants, often with the goal of “following the big players.” Platforms like Profit and Tryd, many of which are built or influenced by Nelogica’s infrastructure, offer features that visualize broker participation and net aggression, aligning with Motion Tracker and Times & Trades tools. These tools exploit the concept of “agressão,” defined as the side that takes the initiative to close a trade by hitting an existing order in the book, thereby consuming liquidity at a given price.

Nelogica’s blog explains that the aggressor in a trade is the player who decides to take the initiative and close the deal at the offered value, consuming liquidity. An aggression on the buy side occurs when there is an order of sale in the book and a player executes a buy by consuming that liquidity at the current ask, lifting the offer; an aggression on the sell side occurs when there is an order of purchase in the book and a player executes a sell by consuming liquidity at the current bid, hitting the bid. The last traded price, where an offer meets an aggressor, dictates the asset’s price path, making the aggressor’s actions central to price formation. Tools such as Times & Trades present detailed information on who bought, who sold, the quantity traded, and, crucially, who was the aggressor in the trade, enabling traders to analyze the “fluxo de agressão” (aggression flow) to identify the actions of principal players.

Nelogica also offers an indicator called “TR – Acúmulo de Agressão – Saldo,” which provides a visual window with the net aggression balance over the analyzed period, essentially a time series of aggregate aggressor-based OFI. This indicator can be interpreted as a cumulative delta of aggressor volume, showing whether net buying aggression or net selling aggression dominates over time. Such tools correspond closely to academic OFI concepts: aggressor-based classification is equivalent to identifying market orders that cross the spread, and net aggressor volume over time is a direct measure of OFI. By tying aggressor identification to broker codes, Brazilian platforms enable users to see not only where aggression is happening but which brokers are responsible, aligning with participant-level microstructure strategies.

### 4.5 B3 Iceberg Detection In Retail Tools

In addition to broker flow and aggression, Brazilian retail platforms sometimes claim to detect iceberg orders (“ofertas iceberg”) or hidden liquidity by analyzing time and sales and order-book patterns. While the search results do not provide detailed documentation of such features, the Motion Tracker description notes that it filters trades to those inside the trading session and can prioritize algorithmic agents, implying some level of algorithm detection and possibly iceberg-related heuristics. Given the international context of iceberg detection methods, it is plausible that Brazilian platforms attempt to infer iceberg behavior from sequences of trades and refills at the same price, similar to synthetic iceberg detection in CME. However, their algorithms are proprietary and not described in academic detail, making reproducibility challenging.

Practically, a B3 researcher could attempt to replicate iceberg detection heuristics by observing whether large trades at a given price are followed by immediate replenishment of depth at that price, beyond what is typical for ordinary market making, and by checking whether cumulative executed volume at that price exceeds typical visible depth. These patterns, combined with broker-tagged information (e.g., if the same broker is repeatedly refilling at the same price), could signal iceberg-like behavior. Nonetheless, without explicit exchange-level iceberg semantics or message flags, precision will be limited, and retail platform claims about iceberg detection should be interpreted cautiously unless their algorithms are made public and validated against ground truth data. The academic iceberg detection literature emphasizes precise rules based on order IDs and trade sizes; where such data are missing, heuristic detection remains an exploratory, not definitive, endeavor.

## 5. Brazilian Retail Platforms: Claims, Documentation, And Reproducibility

### 5.1 Motion Tracker: Monitoring Brokers, Participation, And Saldo

Motion Tracker, an exclusive tool in Nelogica’s Profit Ultra platform, is designed to monitor the positioning and activities of principal market participants in relation to a specific financial asset. It provides valuable information on broker participation, net balance of operations, and average execution prices in real time, aligning closely with the idea of “rastreador de players.” The tool allows users to accompany the actions of major players, including brokers and institutional investors, and to see how these agents interact with the asset, facilitating better understanding of market dynamics and potentially more informed trading decisions. Users can customize the visualization by choosing which players to display, filtering by agents, and ordering the list of players by participation or saldo.

Participation is defined as how much the broker is participating in the asset’s price, likely reflecting their share of volume at current prices, while saldo is the result of buys minus sells executed by the player, representing net position changes. When the list is set to “automatic,” it is ordered by participation for Bovespa assets and by saldo for BM&F assets such as futures, reflecting a differential focus: in equities, participation may be more informative of long-term positioning, while in futures, net position changes (saldo) are more relevant for short-term flow. Motion Tracker computes saldo for a subset of trades, such as a daily accumulation, with filters to include only trades within the session and to exclude trades not originating from algorithms; by default, it uses a “Priorizar Algoritmos Ativos” function to order agents by active algorithms first, then by participation or saldo.

The tool also displays the average execution price of each broker in real time on the right side of the chart, giving users a sense of where brokers’ positions are concentrated. For a B3 research context, Motion Tracker demonstrates that broker-tagged data can be transformed into intuitive visualizations of participant-level order flow and inventory, making it easier to see which brokers are driving current price moves and whether they are net buyers or sellers. However, the underlying algorithms for detecting “algoritmos ativos,” computing participation, and filtering trades are proprietary and not documented in academic sources, limiting direct reproducibility. A researcher can approximate similar features by aggregating trade-level data by broker, computing net volumes and average execution prices, and identifying algorithmic patterns via heuristic filters, but cannot replicate Motion Tracker exactly without access to its code or precise algorithm descriptions.

### 5.2 Times & Trades, Aggressor Flow, And TR – Acúmulo de Agressão

Nelogica’s Times & Trades tool provides a comprehensive view of individual trades, presenting variables such as buyer and seller identities, traded quantity, and aggressor side. By observing who bought, who sold, and who took the initiative in closing the trade (the aggressor), traders can analyze the “fluxo de agressão” with granularity, identifying which brokers are actively consuming liquidity and in which direction. This aligns with academic definitions of aggressive orders as those that cross the spread, lifting the offer or hitting the bid, and passive orders as resting limits that are executed when aggressors arrive. The emphasis on aggressor flow in Brazilian retail practice parallels the focus on OFI in microstructure research, but the terminology is localized (“agressão na compra” and “agressão na venda”).

The TR – Acúmulo de Agressão – Saldo indicator provides a visual window showing the net aggression balance over a period, effectively a time series of cumulative aggressor-based OFI. For example, a positive saldo indicates net buying aggression, suggesting upward pressure, while a negative saldo indicates net selling aggression, suggesting downward pressure. Traders use this indicator to monitor the evolution of aggression and identify divergences between price and aggressor flow—for instance, if price is rising but net aggression is declining, they might anticipate a reversal. As an aggregate, TR – Acúmulo de Agressão is analytically similar to delta-based order-flow imbalance measures used in futures technical analysis, which track net aggressive volume using either tick-based or BVC classification.

From a reproducibility standpoint, TR – Acúmulo de Agressão relies on identification of aggressor side at the trade level, which may be encoded in B3’s trade messages or inferred via quote-based classification algorithms similar to Lee–Ready. A researcher with trade tape and order-book events can replicate aggressor-based OFI by classifying each trade as buy or sell aggression based on whether its price matches the ask or bid and on the sign of price changes, then aggregating net volume over time intervals. This suggests that, unlike Motion Tracker’s algorithm-detection feature, aggressor-flow indicators are reproducible using standard microstructure methods, even if retail platform implementations include proprietary visualization enhancements. The concept of “agressão” itself is essentially an intuitive translation of aggressor vs passive order distinctions in international microstructure theory.

### 5.3 Claims About Algorithm Detection And Hidden Liquidity

Retail platforms sometimes claim to detect algorithmic trading activity and iceberg orders, but such claims are usually marketing-oriented and lack detailed technical documentation. Motion Tracker’s description refers to filters that consider only trades originating from algorithms and a default function that prioritizes active algorithms when ordering agents, implying that the platform differentiates algorithmic from non-algorithmic activity based on patterns or metadata. However, the precise criteria for detecting “algoritmos ativos” are not disclosed: they could involve thresholds on trade frequency, regularity of size and timing, proprietary markers from brokerage feeds, or other features. Without public code or methodological papers, these algorithm-detection claims cannot be rigorously evaluated or reproduced by external researchers.

Similarly, iceberg detection claims, often phrased as “ofertas iceberg” or hidden orders that are inferred from repeated execution patterns at the same price, may rely on heuristic rules akin to synthetic iceberg detection in CME, but are rarely documented. As discussed earlier, robust iceberg detection in academic literature relies on exchange-managed semantics (order IDs, trade volumes exceeding display quantities) and clear update/delete sequences. In markets where such semantics are not explicitly exposed or documented in public feeds, iceberg detection becomes heuristic, and retail platform implementations likely reflect such heuristics. Traders should treat these features as experimental aids rather than precise classifications, and researchers should approach them skeptically unless they can validate the algorithms against known ground truths or detailed technical specifications.

### 5.4 Documentation, Transparency, And Alignment With Academic Signals

Overall, Brazilian retail platforms provide rich visual tools that align intuitively with academic microstructure concepts—order-flow imbalance, aggressor side, broker participation, and hidden liquidity—but their algorithmic underpinnings are often opaque. The Motion Tracker and Times & Trades tools demonstrate how participant codes and aggressor identification can be harnessed to visualize broker-tagged flow and order-flow signals, but they do not publish their classification rules or statistical validation. In contrast, academic work on microprice, OFI, VPIN, and trade classification provides explicit formulas, algorithms, and empirical evaluation, enabling reproducible implementation and rigorous testing.

For a B3 researcher or trader aiming to build robust forecasting signals, the recommended approach is to use academic methodologies as the foundation and to treat retail platform outputs as qualitative guides or inspiration. Broker-tagged flow can be analyzed using participant codes and trade tapes directly, replicating concepts such as participation and saldo; aggressor-based OFI can be computed using Lee–Ready or direct bid–ask matching; microprice and queue-imbalance models can be implemented using L2 depth; and iceberg detection algorithms can be adapted from CME and GLOBEX literature where applicable. Retail platform claims about algorithm detection and iceberg identification can then be benchmarked against these implementations, but should not be relied upon as primary sources of microstructure truth without further validation.

## 6. Conclusion: Signals Most Likely To Add Forecast Skill At A Two-Tick Cost

### 6.1 Synthesizing Evidence Across Iceberg Detection And Order-Flow Signals

The foregoing survey has examined iceberg-order detection methods, order-flow signals with predictive evidence at short horizons, broker-tagged flow analyses, and Brazilian retail platforms’ practices, with a focus on B3 WIN and WDO futures. Iceberg detection on CME and GLOBEX demonstrates that when exchanges manage iceberg orders explicitly and expose order IDs and trade volumes, native iceberg detection can be accomplished via mechanical event-stream rules with high conceptual precision; synthetic iceberg detection is inherently heuristic and depends on patterns in refills and timing. In B3, where FIX specifications enable participant-level identification but iceberg semantics may differ, replicating these methods requires careful inspection of message fields and alignment of trade sizes with resting volumes, but the underlying concepts remain relevant.

Order-flow signals such as static order-book imbalance, microprice, OFI, and queue-imbalance models have strong microstructure foundations and documented predictive power at short horizons. Microprice leverages imbalance and spread to adjust the midprice, yielding a fair-value estimate that often outperforms the simple midprice in predicting the next mid-move. High-resolution microprice models incorporating deeper depth offer further gains by capturing richer supply and demand information across multiple price ranks. OFI, constructed using accurate trade classification methods like Lee–Ready or tick rules, reflects net aggressive pressure and predicts short-term price changes, especially when combined with static imbalance. Queue-imbalance models provide probabilistic timing of price moves based on birth–death processes of limit and market orders, reinforcing the importance of queue sizes and flows.

VPIN and flow toxicity metrics present a more nuanced picture. While they are conceptually appealing and have attracted regulatory and practitioner attention, empirical critiques highlight that VPIN’s incremental predictive power disappears once controls for volume and volatility are applied and that its behavior is highly sensitive to trade classification, especially when using BVC. This suggests that VPIN may be more useful as a diagnostic tool for extreme conditions than as a primary forecasting signal for ordinary trading environments.

Broker-tagged flow analyses, enabled by B3’s participant identifiers and exploited by tools like Motion Tracker and Times & Trades, provide an important dimension to microstructure research. They allow decomposition of aggregate flows into broker-specific strategies and facilitate “follow the informed trader” and herding detection approaches. Retail practices around “agressão,” “fluxo de corretoras,” and “rastreador de players” are intuitive translations of academic OFI and participant-level microstructure concepts, though their implementations are proprietary and require statistical validation. Hidden-liquidity and iceberg detection claims in retail platforms should be treated cautiously, as they are likely heuristic and undocumented.

### 6.2 Three Signals Likely To Add Forecast Skill At A Two-Tick Cost

Given the available evidence and microstructure theory, three signals stand out as particularly promising for adding forecast skill at a cost of approximately two ticks in B3 WIN/WDO futures, assuming access to historical order-book events and broker-tagged trade tapes.

The first is a high-resolution microprice signal that incorporates multi-level order-book imbalances and spread information, building on Stoikov’s microprice framework and recent error-correcting models. This signal would be constructed by computing the classical top-of-book microprice \(P_{\text{micro}} = M + g(I, S)\), where \(I\) is static imbalance and \(S\) is the spread, and then adjusting it using learned functions of deeper-level imbalances, as in the Tsetlin machine-based high-resolution microprice model. The data requirement is a full L2 depth feed with at least several levels on each side, along with time stamps, allowing the reconstruction of imbalance vectors and spread dynamics. Because microprice approximates the expected future midprice conditioned on current book state and has documented superiority over midprice as a short-horizon predictor, using deviations between actual midprice and high-resolution microprice as signals can provide directional edge that, when managed carefully, justifies crossing the spread or incurring slippage of about two ticks in expectation.

The second signal is accurately classified order-flow imbalance at the trade level, focusing on aggressor-based OFI over 1–5 minute windows. This involves classifying each trade as buyer-initiated or seller-initiated using Lee–Ready algorithms or direct bid–ask matching and computing net aggressive volume (buy minus sell) over rolling windows. Data requirements include tick-level trade tape with prices, sizes, and either aggressor identifiers or contemporaneous bid–ask quotes, and optionally limit and cancel messages to incorporate passive flows. The resulting OFI signal reflects dynamic pressure and has established predictive relationships with short-term price changes, especially in liquid futures markets. Combining OFI with microprice yields a robust two-dimensional signal: microprice provides fair value, and OFI provides incremental directional pressure; trades can be executed when both signals align strongly, increasing the chance that forecasted moves exceed two ticks after costs.

The third signal is broker-tagged aggressor imbalance for a small set of persistently informed or dominant participants, inspired by Motion Tracker’s participation and saldo features and by flow toxicity research. This signal would be constructed by aggregating aggressor-based OFI by broker over short intervals, identifying brokers whose net flows exhibit strong predictive relationships with future prices, and then tracking those brokers in real time. Data requirements include trade tapes with buyer and seller broker codes, participant identifiers in order messages, and aggressor classification. Such a signal leverages asymmetric information: if certain brokers represent institutional or algorithmic traders with superior information or execution capabilities, their flows are more likely to be informed and to drive price changes, making “follow the informed trader” strategies viable. By focusing on a small set of brokers with demonstrated persistence, one can reduce noise and capture sharper signals that may justify a two-tick cost.

### 6.3 Data Requirements And Integration For B3 WIN/WDO

Implementing these three signals in B3 WIN/WDO futures requires a coherent data architecture. High-resolution microprice demands multi-level L2 depth snapshots or event-level LOB messages that can be aggregated into depth profiles, along with accurate time stamps and spread measurements. Order-flow imbalance requires tick-level trade tapes with prices, sizes, and either aggressor-side flags or the ability to reconstruct aggressor side via bid–ask quotes and Lee–Ready classification. Broker-tagged signals require the preservation of buyer and seller broker codes, participant identifiers, and possibly algorithmic markers in FIX fields and downstream data feeds. Iceberg detection, while valuable for reconstructing hidden liquidity, is a complementary task: if B3 exposes iceberg semantics or message-level hidden-volume flags, CME and GLOBEX detection methods can be adapted; if not, synthetic iceberg heuristics can be explored, but should be treated as exploratory and not as primary signals.

Integrating these signals into trading strategies entails combining static and dynamic information: high-resolution microprice provides a continuous fair-value estimate; OFI provides incremental pressure over short windows; broker-tagged OFI provides cross-sectional information on who is driving pressure. For example, a strategy might take long positions when microprice lies significantly above the current midprice, OFI is strongly positive, and one or more identified “informed” brokers are net buyers; conversely, it might take short positions when microprice lies below midprice, OFI is negative, and informed brokers are net sellers. The expected edge from such confluence events may exceed two ticks, making them potentially profitable after considering spread and slippage. Robust backtesting, out-of-sample validation, and careful risk management are essential, but the microstructure evidence surveyed here suggests that these three signals are among the most promising for B3 WIN/WDO futures at 1–5 minute horizons.

In conclusion, the convergence of academic microstructure research and Brazilian retail practices provides a rich toolkit for B3 traders and researchers. By grounding signal construction in well-documented methods—microprice, OFI, and broker-tagged flow—and by critically evaluating proprietary platform claims, one can build forecasting and execution strategies that leverage the structural features of B3’s limit order book and participant architecture, aiming to capture short-horizon edges that justify modest transaction costs.
