# Short-Horizon Forecasting of Index and FX Futures: Methods and Evidence up to 2026

Short-horizon forecasting of index and foreign-exchange (FX) futures—on horizons of one to five trading days—is a demanding test bed for modern machine learning, because signals are weak, markets are noisy, and transaction costs quickly erode any marginal edge. Across the literature up to 2026, the strongest out-of-sample, net-of-cost evidence for tradable predictability tends to come from gradient-boosting and related supervised learning methods applied to rich, carefully engineered feature sets, especially those incorporating sentiment and cross-asset information, while deep sequence models such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and hybrid architectures also produce high risk-adjusted returns in some futures datasets when coupled to realistic walk-forward backtests and transaction-cost controls. Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) like Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, and Moirai-2 show promising results as pretrained priors and as components inside larger decision systems, but the most careful equity-return studies find that, at daily horizons, their out-of-sample \(R^2\) and skill relative to random-walk and strong supervised baselines remain small, and often economically negligible once plausible costs are considered. Constituent-to-index approaches—where one forecasts index futures using information from component assets such as commodities, spot indices, FX pairs, or macro sentiment—provide several compelling case studies on S&P 500 and CSI 300 futures, but there is still limited published evidence specifically focused on 1–5-day horizons for Brazilian instruments such as Ibovespa, WIN (mini index futures), and WDO (mini dollar futures, especially when evaluated under purged, walk-forward protocols and net of trading frictions.  

## Scope, Assets, and Evaluation Principles

### Short-Horizon Futures and FX Return Forecasting

The central focus of this survey is 1-to-5-day forecasting of returns on index futures and FX futures, which are among the most liquid and institutionally important derivatives contracts. Index futures such as S&P 500, Nasdaq 100, CSI 300, and Ibovespa futures provide leveraged exposure to broad equity markets, while FX futures and forwards track currency pairs like EUR/USD and USD/JPY. On these horizons, returns are typically close to unpredictable under simple models, with random-walk and white-noise baselines often performing surprisingly well, which makes any statistically significant and economically meaningful edge difficult to obtain and harder still to sustain out of sample. This difficulty is exacerbated in FX markets, which practitioners and researchers alike describe as path-dependent, structurally unstable, and sensitive to the sequence of information arrival rather than simple levels of macro variables, limiting the persistence of predictive relationships.  

Within this challenging landscape, researchers have explored several broad methodological families. Gradient boosting and related tree ensembles operate on engineered features derived from prices, volumes, technical indicators, sentiment indices, and macro variables, and are valued for their robustness, interpretability, and strong performance in tabular, heterogeneous data. Deep learning approaches—including LSTMs, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and hybrid architectures linking variable-selection networks to recurrent layers—offer flexible sequence modelling and have recently been benchmarked at scale for daily futures prediction and position sizing. Time-series foundation models (TSFMs), exemplified by Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, and Moirai-2, adopt a pretrained, often transformer-based approach: they ingest multiple time series and sometimes covariates, then emit probabilistic forecasts over future values in a zero-shot or lightly fine-tuned manner. Finally, constituent-to-index methods aim to forecast index futures using information from their parts, whether index components, related commodities, FX pairs, or interest-rate futures, often exploiting economic linkages such as discount-rate and cash-flow channels.  

Because the query explicitly emphasizes what works out of sample net of costs, it is crucial to distinguish between purely statistical metrics (such as \(R^2\), RMSE, MAPE, or hit-rate) and economic performance measures like Sharpe ratio, breakeven transaction-cost buffers, and cost-adjusted cumulative returns. Several papers report high in-sample fit or classification accuracy but either do not implement realistic walk-forward protocols or ignore slippage, bid–ask spreads, and fees, making their findings fragile from a trading perspective. Others adopt more rigorous rolling-origin or expanding-window evaluations, often with purged cross-validation to avoid look-ahead and leakage, and explicitly estimate the impact of transaction costs on strategy profitability. Throughout this survey, studies that lack walk-forward or purged evaluation are explicitly flagged, and special attention is given to those that assess net-of-cost feasibility.  

### Asset Coverage and Horizon Alignment

A practical limitation of the published literature is that relatively few studies are targeted precisely at 1–5-day horizons for index and FX futures with full trading-cost treatment; instead, many papers examine daily horizons with implicit one-day holding periods, or slightly longer horizons such as 10-day volatility forecasts, monthly index forecasts, or next-day FX returns. Nonetheless, these settings are close enough to the requested 1–5-day range that their methodologies and conclusions are informative. For example, volatility-forecasting studies on S&P 500 and CSI 300 index futures using LSTM and artificial neural networks (ANNs) apply 5- and 10-day horizons when predicting return volatilities, which directly influence risk-adjusted returns on corresponding futures strategies. Similarly, deep-learning benchmarks for daily futures prediction optimize Sharpe ratio across a cross-asset dataset covering commodities, equity indices, bonds, and FX futures from 2010–2025, effectively focusing on one-day ahead returns.  

In FX, much of the systematic literature centers on daily spot returns, but many of the methods—including LSTMs, ANN ensembles, and boosting—are directly applicable to FX futures via the tight coupling between futures prices and spot exchange rates, particularly for highly traded pairs like EUR/USD and USD/JPY. Additional work explicitly targets S&P 500 index futures and 10-year U.S. Treasury futures using sentiment-enhanced models, offering clean examples of futures return prediction at daily horizons. For Brazil, the published research on Ibovespa focuses mostly on monthly horizons rather than the intraday or few-day horizons relevant to WIN and WDO, highlighting a gap between academic studies and the practitioner emphasis on very short-term index and FX futures trading.  

### Evaluation Protocols: Walk-Forward vs Static Splits

The reliability of any claimed edge depends critically on the evaluation protocol. Standard static train–test splits can conceal significant look-ahead bias and regime dependence, especially in financial time series with structural breaks and non-stationarity. In recognition of this, several recent papers adopt rolling-origin (walk-forward) procedures, in which models are trained on data up to a given time and then tested on subsequent periods, rolling the origin forward as time advances. The Chronos-2 financial forecasting study, for example, evaluates multivariate and univariate forecasts for equities and U.S. Treasury rates using rolling monthly origin evaluations from 2000–2025, thereby respecting temporal ordering and capturing evolving regimes. Similarly, the benchmark of pretrained TSFMs against train-from-scratch neural baselines on U.S. equity returns uses a rolling-origin protocol with an equalized context budget, comparing models against random-walk benchmarks under realistic sequential updating.  

In the macro alpha news sentiment case study, daily sentiment indices built from the GDELT news database feed an XGBoost classifier, which is evaluated through five-fold expanding-window cross-validation, explicitly designed to emulate walk-forward learning and ensure that out-of-sample predictions reflect genuine forecasting rather than in-sample fit. Large-scale deep learning benchmarks for daily futures prediction also adopt unified protocols, applying consistent rolling and cross-validation structures across architectures to evaluate risk-adjusted performance on a diversified cross-asset dataset. In contrast, several earlier machine learning studies on Ibovespa and gold prices rely on static train–test splits or simple cross-validation without purging overlapping information across folds, which can inflates apparent \(R^2\), classification accuracy, or Sharpe ratios. These latter studies are flagged below as lacking fully purged, walk-forward evaluation.  

## Gradient Boosting and Engineered Features for Short-Horizon Futures and FX Prediction

### Conceptual Overview of Gradient Boosting in Financial Time Series

Gradient boosting, including implementations such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, operates by sequentially fitting decision trees to residuals from previous trees, thereby focusing the ensemble on difficult-to-predict portions of the data. These methods are particularly well suited to tabular financial datasets where features are heterogeneous, potentially non-linear, and include complex interactions among prices, volumes, technical indicators, sentiment scores, and macroeconomic variables. Because boosting algorithms can flexibly capture non-linear relationships while incorporating regularization to mitigate overfitting, they have become standard baselines for many financial forecasting tasks, including index futures returns, FX returns, and volatility prediction.  

A key design choice in boosting-based forecasting systems is the feature set and temporal structure. For 1–5-day horizons on futures and FX markets, features often include recent lagged returns, moving averages, volatility measures, technical indicators such as MACD and Williams %R, cross-asset signals (e.g., other indices or commodities), and sentiment indices derived from news or social media, sometimes augmented with volatility-adjusted or carry variables. Feature horizons typically span from a few days up to several months, with researchers balancing the desire to capture medium-term dependencies against the risk of introducing stale information that no longer carries predictive power. Evaluation protocols must be walk-forward or expanding-window to avoid look-ahead bias, especially when features involve rolling statistics or sentiment measures that are themselves estimated from historical data.

### News Sentiment and Macro Alpha: XGBoost for FX and Treasury Futures

Another compelling example of gradient boosting on engineered features, explicitly evaluated net of costs, is the interpretable machine learning framework for macro alpha based on global news sentiment. In this study, the author processes the Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone (GDELT), a worldwide news feed, using FinBERT—a finance-specific BERT model—to compute daily sentiment indices capturing mean tone, dispersion, and event impact. These indices serve as features for an XGBoost classifier tasked with predicting next-day returns for EUR/USD, USD/JPY, and 10-year U.S. Treasury futures (ZN), thereby directly addressing short-horizon FX and bond futures forecasting.  

The dataset covers an out-of-sample period from around 2017 through April 2025, with earlier data used for training and validation, and the evaluation protocol employs five-fold expanding-window cross-validation. This design simulates walk-forward learning: models are trained on an initial window, then tested on subsequent periods, with the training window expanding over time; cross-validation folds are constructed to avoid leakage, enabling robust performance estimation. Predictive performance is assessed not merely by classification metrics but via cost-adjusted trading strategies, in which predicted signals drive long–short positions in FX and Treasury futures, and transaction costs are explicitly deducted.  

The reported results are striking: out-of-sample Sharpe ratios reach 5.87 for EUR/USD, 4.65 for USD/JPY, and 4.65 for 10-year Treasury futures, with compound annual growth rates exceeding 50% in FX and over 22% in bonds, after accounting for transaction costs. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis identifies sentiment dispersion and article impact as key predictive features, reinforcing the interpretability of the gradient-boosting model. This study provides one of the clearest demonstrations that, under carefully designed feature engineering and evaluation protocols, XGBoost can deliver robust, net-of-cost short-horizon predictability in FX and Treasury futures, with the daily horizon aligning directly with 1-day forecasts and strategies that could be adapted to 1–5-day holding periods.  

### Meta-Analysis of Machine Learning for FX: Boosting and Ensembles

A systematic literature review (SLR) of FX market forecasting covering sixty machine learning studies between 2010 and 2021 further supports the strong performance of gradient boosting and related ensemble methods relative to classical time-series approaches. The review notes that machine-learning algorithms, including support vector machines (SVM), deep learning models, and hybrid ensembles, generally outperform traditional stochastic methods when forecasting FX returns. While many models focus on daily horizons and sometimes longer windows, the meta-analysis emphasizes that advanced exogenous financial information combined with discriminative committee methods—such as SVM, Relevance Vector Machines, and neural networks—perform well in estimating daily FX carry basket returns.  

In terms of evaluation metrics, the SLR finds that MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and MSE are the most commonly used measures, with EUR/USD being the most frequently studied currency pair. Notably, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and LSTMs tend to outperform feed-forward neural networks and SVMs on average, implying exploitable temporal relationships in FX time series across asset classes and countries. While gradient boosting is not highlighted as the single dominant method, ensemble approaches and hybrid models that incorporate boosting components are often among the strongest performers, particularly when evaluated on out-of-sample data with realistic train–test splits. However, the SLR also observes that many reviewed studies do not explicitly account for transaction costs or use purged cross-validation, meaning that some reported edges may be fragile when implemented in live trading.

## Time-Series Foundation Models: Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2, and Beyond

### Architectural Overview and Financial Applications

Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) represent a newer paradigm in financial forecasting, drawing inspiration from large language models but tailored to sequential numerical data. Chronos-2, Amazon’s successor to the original Chronos models, exemplifies this approach: it tokenizes continuous time-series values by scaling and quantizing them into a discrete vocabulary, then uses an encoder-only transformer derived from T5 to predict the next tokens, emitting probabilistic forecasts over time. Architecturally, Chronos-2 introduces a group-attention mechanism that supports in-context learning across sets of series, enabling joint inference across related univariate and multivariate time series without task-specific retraining.  

TimesFM 2.5, developed by Google, similarly adopts a transformer-based architecture optimized for time series, with tooling that integrates well into enterprise data platforms like BigQuery and Google Cloud. Moirai-2, from Salesforce, is particularly strong in handling multi-asset portfolios, emphasizing flexible multivariate modelling and covariate integration. These models are typically pretrained on large collections of time series from diverse domains, including synthetic data, financial datasets, and industrial measurements, and are designed to perform zero-shot or few-shot forecasting across a wide range of tasks.  

In finance, TSFMs have been applied to tasks such as equity return forecasting, volatility prediction, and multi-asset futures forecasting. However, the evidence regarding their performance relative to strong supervised baselines like gradient boosting and specialized deep-learning architectures is mixed, particularly on noisy, low-signal problems such as daily equity returns. While TSFMs often achieve high rankings in benchmark leaderboards, the economic significance of their gains over random-walk or simple linear models can be modest, especially when trading costs are taken into account.  

### Chronos-2 in Economic and Financial Forecasting: Levels and Rates

A dedicated study evaluates Chronos-2 as a foundation-model approach to economic and financial forecasting, with a specific focus on whether multivariate inputs improve accuracy relative to univariate baselines. The datasets include panels of Magnificent-7 equities, U.S. Treasury interest rates, and a combined panel mixing equity and rate series, with rolling monthly evaluations from 2000 to 2025. Forecasting targets are primarily levels (prices or yields) rather than returns, and the horizons vary with input window lengths and forecast horizons, with performance assessed using RMSE and MAPE.  

The central hypothesis is that multivariate inputs, when properly chosen and aligned, allow Chronos-2 to extract cross-series information and outperform purely univariate baselines. The empirical results support this hypothesis: across datasets, multivariate forecasts consistently outperform univariate forecasts, with particularly strong gains for interest rates and meaningful improvements for equities. Series-level comparisons show improvements under multivariate inputs in every case, and error dispersion is generally lower, indicating more stable forecasting. However, mixing time series across equity and interest-rate markets reduces forecast accuracy, suggesting that adding noisy or weakly related context can degrade performance, an important caution for practitioners.  

While these results demonstrate the value of multivariate modelling and cross-series attention in TSFMs, the study does not explicitly address trading strategies or net-of-cost returns, nor does it focus on 1–5-day horizons; instead, it emphasizes monthly rolling forecasts of levels. As such, Chronos-2’s superiority over univariate baselines in this setting speaks to its statistical forecasting power rather than its economic value in short-horizon futures or FX trading. Nonetheless, the multivariate group-attention mechanism could, in principle, be applied to daily index and FX futures data, potentially leveraging cross-series correlations for improved short-horizon forecasting.  

### Pretrained TSFMs for Financial Return Forecasting: Equity Daily Returns

More directly relevant to short-horizon return forecasting is the study of pretrained TSFMs for financial return forecasting, which benchmarks TSFMs against train-from-scratch neural baselines in a conservative equity setting. This paper evaluates TimeGPT/TimeGPT-LH, TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2.0, Chronos, and Chronos-2 against deep-learning baselines such as NBEATS, NHITS, PatchTST, iTransformer, and Kernel Attention Networks (KAN) on five liquid U.S. equities: Apple (AAPL), Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOG), JPMorgan (JPM), and Meta (META). The forecasting targets are daily linear and log returns, and models are compared under an equalized context budget, a rolling-origin protocol, and random-walk benchmarks.  

The study provides a theoretical framing of pretraining as an inductive prior, linking PAC-Bayes transfer intuition, information-theoretic predictability limits, and attention geometry, thereby clarifying why strong model rankings need not imply economically meaningful predictability in noisy markets. Empirically, pretrained TSFMs dominate the model ranking distribution, accounting for eight of ten task-level wins when evaluated by statistical metrics such as RMSE and MAPE. Moirai-2.0 and TimesFM-2.5 achieve the strongest average ranks, leading tasks for AAPL, JPM, GOOG, and AMZN, while Chronos wins the remaining AMZN task; however, the iTransformer baseline wins both META tasks, indicating that local supervised learning can still outperform generic pretraining for specific assets.  

Crucially, gains over the random-walk benchmark are described as small and sparse. A one-sided Diebold–Mariano test rejects equal or inferior predictive accuracy only for Chronos on AMZN and Moirai-2.0 on GOOG, highlighting that statistically significant improvements over random-walk are limited to a minority of tasks. The authors conclude that TSFMs serve as useful practical priors that reduce model-development costs in low-data financial forecasting but are not universal engines for statistically reliable alpha generation in realistic deployment. The study does not translate these results into trading performance or net-of-cost returns; however, the small statistical gains suggest that, once transaction costs are considered, economic benefits may be modest or even negligible on daily equity returns, and by extension, similar caution may apply to index futures and FX futures on comparable horizons.  

### Benchmarking TSFMs vs Traditional Models: TimesFM vs Chronos vs Moirai

An independent comparative resource examines TimesFM 2.5, Chronos-2, and Moirai-2 for financial forecasting, emphasizing architectural trade-offs and practical considerations. The GIFT-Eval benchmark, comprising ninety-seven task configurations, reportedly shows Chronos-2 with the highest overall win rate across tasks, though the ranking shifts depending on data frequency, asset class, and forecast horizon. Key findings from the literature include an evaluation of TSFMs on US Treasury yields, EUR/USD volatility, and equity spreads, in which pretrained models required three to ten fewer years of data to reach comparable accuracy relative to traditional specialist models. However, in two of three tasks, traditional specialist models still matched or exceeded TSFM performance, underscoring that pretraining is not a universal guarantee of superiority.  

The comparative analysis also notes that off-the-shelf TSFMs often underperform on financial data when trained or evaluated under generic settings, and that finance-native pretraining delivers substantial gains compared with purely generic training. Few-shot fine-tuning on specific asset classes consistently outperforms zero-shot inference for financial applications, suggesting that TSFMs should be viewed more as adaptable priors than as plug-and-play predictors. Importantly, the resource warns that TSFM evaluations sometimes unintentionally include test datasets overlapping with pretraining data, inflating accuracy by 47–184% and complicating fair assessment. As a result, practitioners are advised to verify that their test data lies outside the model’s pretraining corpus, and to interpret high leaderboard scores cautiously, especially in low-signal domains like short-horizon returns.  

From the perspective of 1–5-day index and FX futures forecasting, these lessons imply that TSFMs are promising tools for exploratory modelling and rapid prototyping, but they should be complemented by structured fine-tuning and careful out-of-sample validation against strong baselines such as gradient boosting and specialized deep-learning architectures. Zero-shot TSFM performance on raw return series, particularly at daily horizons, is unlikely to deliver large, robust net-of-cost alpha without further adaptation.  

### TSFMs in Trading Systems: LLM Decision Enhancement and Text-Augmented Models

Beyond stand-alone forecasting, TSFMs have been used as components within larger trading systems. A recent study investigates whether incorporating TSFM forecasts improves the decisions of a large language model (LLM) trading agent. The agent is augmented with predictions from multiple state-of-the-art TSFMs, including Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2, and a model called Toto, and is evaluated on 30-day cumulative returns over multiple backend runs. Table 2 in the paper shows that baseline LLM-only trading returns exhibit small dispersion across four TSFM backends, indicating that the baseline agent is stable enough to serve as a reference. The main question is whether adding TSFM-derived forecasts in various formats (raw price trajectories, return ratios, quantile forecasts) yields statistically significant improvements.  

The results demonstrate that TSFM forecasts consistently deliver statistically significant and practically meaningful improvements in LLM-based trading performance, with p-values below \(3.3 \times 10^{-4}\) and moderate effect sizes (Cohen’s \(d_z = 0.42\)). Although the asset universe and exact horizon are not fully detailed in the excerpt, the study focuses on 30-day trading performance and shows that TSFM-enhanced agents achieve higher cumulative returns than the LLM-only baseline. Transaction costs are not explicitly discussed, but the emphasis on cumulative returns and dispersion suggests that the evaluation includes some notion of realistic market variability. This work illustrates that TSFM forecasts, while modest on their own, can meaningfully enhance decision quality when integrated into larger reasoning systems, a promising avenue for applications on index and FX futures where the LLM can combine TSFM predictions with broader context.  

Another line of research, the FTS-Text-MoE model, combines time-series foundation models with text inputs such as news and tweets to predict financial time-series trends. The model outperforms traditional benchmarks in terms of investment returns and Sharpe ratio, demonstrating superior accuracy and an ability to predict future market trends, albeit with increased return volatility due to its responsiveness to time-series fluctuations. When compared directly to Chronos and Moirai, FTS-Text-MoE shows improved performance with the addition of text inputs but does not surpass Chronos and Moirai in all industries and scenarios; the latter models exhibit more conservative prediction approaches, smoothing curves to reduce errors and maintain lower MSE and MAE. These comparisons suggest that TSFMs can form robust backbones for text-augmented forecasting systems, but that hybrid architectures may be better suited for aggressive trend exploitation at the expense of higher volatility.  

### Limitations and Absence of Clear Positive Net-of-Cost Evidence on Daily Returns

Despite architectural sophistication and promising benchmark performance, the evidence so far indicates that TSFMs are not yet clear winners for 1–5-day index and FX futures returns in net-of-cost terms. The equity-return benchmarking study finds that TSFMs rarely deliver statistically significant improvements over random-walk on daily returns, and does not translate these modest gains into trading strategies or net-of-cost performance. Comparative resources note that off-the-shelf TSFMs often underperform finance-specific models and require careful fine-tuning to approach parity, particularly when evaluated on noisy financial datasets.  

While TSFMs appreciably improve LLM decision-making and can be competitive in text-augmented systems, these setups are more complex than straightforward return forecasting, and their performance is tied to the behaviour of the surrounding decision logic as much as to the TSFM itself. Moreover, none of the cited TSFM studies explicitly present net-of-cost, short-horizon trading results on index or FX futures under purged walk-forward evaluation. As a consequence, the current state of evidence up to 2026 suggests that TSFMs should be viewed as flexible priors and components within larger systems rather than as individual engines of reliable short-horizon alpha in futures markets.

## Constituent-to-Index and Cross-Series Forecasting

### Conceptual Rationale: Forecasting the Parts to Improve the Whole

Constituent-to-index forecasting refers to the practice of predicting an index futures contract by modelling its constituent elements—either the index’s component stocks or economically linked assets such as commodities, currency pairs, and interest-rate futures. The rationale is that index returns reflect both aggregate cash flows and discount rates, which are themselves influenced by underlying assets and macroeconomic conditions. For instance, commodity prices influence corporate profitability and inflation expectations, affecting equity index valuations through both cash-flow and discount-rate channels; FX movements alter competitiveness and earnings; and interest-rate futures encode expectations about monetary policy, which directly influence discount factors.  

From a modelling standpoint, constituent-to-index approaches can be implemented either through multivariate time-series models that jointly forecast multiple related series and then aggregate them, or through feature-based models where constituent information appears as exogenous predictors for the index futures. TSFMs like Chronos-2, which support group attention across related series, are naturally suited for multivariate constituent modelling; gradient boosting models can incorporate constituent features directly; and hybrid systems may combine both. The theoretical question is whether forecasting the parts yields materially better index forecasts than modelling the index alone, particularly on short horizons where noise is high and cross-asset correlations may be unstable.  

### Multivariate Chronos-2 across Equities and Rates

The Chronos-2 multivariate finance study provides direct evidence that joint modelling of related series—such as multiple equities or multiple Treasury yields—improves forecasting accuracy relative to univariate modelling of each series separately. Across panels of Magnificent-7 equities and U.S. Treasury interest rates, multivariate inputs enable Chronos-2 to exploit cross-series dependencies and reduce forecast errors, as measured by MAPE and RMSE. Series-level comparisons show that every asset benefits from multivariate modelling, and error dispersion (standard deviation of forecast errors) is smaller under multivariate inputs, indicating more stable performance.  

However, the same study finds that mixing equities and interest rates within a single multivariate panel reduces forecast accuracy, implying that cross-series information is beneficial primarily when it arises from closely related or homogeneous assets. This finding speaks directly to constituent-to-index forecasting: modelling an equity index’s component stocks together may yield gains, but indiscriminately adding unrelated assets like distant commodities or unrelated FX pairs could introduce noise. From a short-horizon perspective, the implication is that carefully selected constituent sets, with strong economic linkages and stable correlations, are likely to produce more reliable index futures forecasts than broad, heterogeneous multivariate panels.  

### Exogenous Variables for Ibovespa: Cross-Market Features

A Brazil-focused study explores the use of exogenous variables for long-term forecasts of the Ibovespa index. The methodology adopts artificial neural networks to consolidate, select, and model variables that encapsulate fifteen years of information on the Brazilian financial market, including prices of products sold by companies listed on the B3 exchange, performance indicators of the economy and foreign trade, measures of public-sector imbalances, inflation levels, interest rates, and other stock indices. These exogenous variables function as constituents in an extended sense: they reflect the broader economic and financial environment that drives Ibovespa’s cash flows and discount rates.  

The forecasting horizon in this study is twelve months ahead, rather than 1–5 days, and the model is trained on monthly data. Nevertheless, the results demonstrate that an artificial neural network can predict the future performance of Ibovespa with over 85% explanatory power of the selected variables, even during periods of high market volatility. This suggests that constituent-like exogenous variables carry significant predictive information at longer horizons. However, the evaluation protocol is not fully detailed; it appears to rely on static train–test splits, and there is no discussion of walk-forward validation or transaction costs. As such, while this study underscores the value of cross-series and exogenous variables for index forecasting, its direct relevance to short-horizon, net-of-cost index futures trading is limited, and it must be flagged as lacking walk-forward evaluation.  

### Spot–Futures Arbitrage on CSI 300: Constituent Arbitrage Index

A particularly innovative constituent-based approach involves forecasting spot–futures arbitrage opportunities for the CSI 300 index using machine learning. The study begins by defining a non-arbitrage interval for index futures relative to the spot index, based on theoretical bounds derived from cost-of-carry and other considerations. It then constructs an index that measures the relative position of the futures price within this interval, effectively capturing the degree of mispricing between spot and futures.  

This arbitrage index serves as the foundation for a trading strategy: exchange-traded fund (ETF)-based portfolios are built to track the CSI 300 spot index with minimal tracking error, and a strategy consisting of non-arbitrage intervals and unwinding timing indicators is derived. Machine learning methods—including LASSO, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Back Propagation Neural Networks (BPNN), and LSTM—are used to forecast the arbitrage indicator based on historical high-frequency data. The aim is to predict when spot–futures mispricing will revert, enabling profitable arbitrage trades.  

The empirical results show that LSTM outperforms all other algorithms over the entire sample period, achieving an RMSE of 0.00813, a MAPE of 0.70%, and an \(R^2\) of 92.09%, along with an arbitrage return of 58.18%. Interestingly, in shorter subperiods and specific market conditions (bull or bear markets), LASSO can outperform, demonstrating that simpler models may excel in local regimes. The evaluation protocol involves backtesting the arbitrage strategy over time, respecting temporal ordering, and measuring realized arbitrage returns. However, the excerpt does not specify the detailed handling of transaction costs; given the nature of arbitrage trades, it is likely that at least some cost considerations are implicitly included in the return calculations, but explicit cost parameterization is not described.  

From a constituent-to-index perspective, this study illustrates how modelling the relationship between an index futures and its spot index—a key constituent in the derivative pricing relationship—can yield significant economic gains. The arbitrage index itself can be viewed as an engineered feature summarizing cross-series information between spot and futures, and its predictive modelling via machine learning provides a strong example of cross-series forecasting in index futures markets.

## Evaluation Protocols, Skill Measures, and Transaction Costs

### Importance of Walk-Forward and Purged Evaluation

Across all methodological families, the most robust evidence for tradable forecasting in financial markets comes from studies that use walk-forward or expanding-window evaluation protocols and explicitly guard against data leakage. Rolling-origin procedures, as used in the TSFM equity-return benchmarking, ensure that models are trained only on historical data at each point and tested on subsequent periods, respecting the natural temporal ordering. Expanding-window cross-validation, as in the macro news sentiment study, provides multiple overlapping training and test splits that mimic walk-forward learning while enabling more precise estimates of out-of-sample performance.  

Purged cross-validation is particularly important when using overlapping windows of returns or when features are constructed from rolling statistics, as in technical indicators or sentiment measures. Without purging, information from the test period can indirectly influence training through overlapping windows, producing artificially optimistic estimates of performance. Large-scale deep-learning benchmarks for daily futures prediction emphasize unified experimental frameworks spanning fifteen years of data, multiple asset classes, and heterogeneous regimes, evaluated under consistent rolling protocols that minimize such biases.  

In contrast, many earlier or narrower studies rely on static train–test splits, dividing the dataset once into a training portion and a test portion, without rolling or purging. While such splits can be informative, they are more vulnerable to regime-specific effects and may overstate performance if the test period happens to be particularly favourable or similar to the training period. For short-horizon futures and FX forecasting, where regimes and patterns change rapidly, walk-forward evaluation is essential to demonstrate genuine out-of-sample skill.  

### Statistical Skill Measures: \(R^2\), RMSE, MAPE

Common statistical measures used in financial forecasting include \(R^2\) (coefficient of determination), RMSE (root mean squared error), MAPE (mean absolute percentage error), MAE (mean absolute error), and MSE (mean squared error). High \(R^2\) values in studies such as Ibovespa exogenous-variable forecasts or gold price prediction (with reported \(R^2\) values above 0.85 and up to 0.9993) suggest strong fit in-sample or under static evaluation. Similarly, low RMSE and MAPE values in CSI 300 arbitrage prediction and volatility forecasting indicate accurate estimation of target variables.  

However, high statistical fit does not necessarily translate into tradable edge, particularly on short horizons. For example, the TSFM equity-return study finds that TSFMs achieve strong ranking performance and low RMSE relative to other models, yet gains over random-walk are small and sparse, and only two tasks show statistically significant improvement under Diebold–Mariano tests. This underscores that even substantial reductions in error metrics may not yield economically meaningful improvements in returns once costs and risk are considered. Moreover, for daily and 1–5-day horizons, the theoretical predictability limits implied by information-theoretic arguments suggest that any achievable \(R^2\) is likely to be small, and differences between models may be more about robustness than magnitude.  

### Economic Performance Measures: Sharpe Ratios, Transaction Costs, and Breakeven Buffers

Economic performance measures are critical for evaluating the practical viability of forecasting models. The Sharpe ratio, which measures average excess return per unit of volatility, is widely used to assess risk-adjusted performance, especially in backtests of trading strategies. For instance, the deep CNN futures model achieves significantly higher average annual returns and Sharpe ratios than previous approaches based on nearest neighbour and SVM, with improvements in Sharpe ratio ranging from 53% to 199% over benchmarks. Similarly, deep-learning benchmarks for daily futures prediction optimize Sharpe ratio directly, comparing architectures under out-of-sample performance metrics that include robustness to downside risk and transaction costs.  

In the macro news sentiment study, cost-adjusted Sharpe ratios of 5.87 for EUR/USD and 4.65 for USD/JPY and Treasury futures demonstrate exceptional economic performance even after accounting for trading frictions. Compound annual growth rates above 50% in FX and over 22% in bonds further confirm the strength of the strategy. Conversely, many studies focus on statistical metrics or gross returns without explicitly subtracting costs, leaving open the question of whether reported returns would survive realistic trading expenses.  

Transaction costs can be approximated through breakeven cost buffers, which estimate the maximum allowable costs for a strategy to remain profitable. In the deep-learning benchmark, architectures such as xLSTM demonstrate large breakeven transaction-cost buffers, indicating improved robustness to trading frictions. This kind of analysis is essential for short-horizon futures and FX strategies, which often involve frequent trading and high turnover. Without explicit cost adjustment or breakeven analysis, claims of profitability may be misleading.

## Conclusion: What Works for 1–5-Day Index and FX Futures Forecasting as of 2026

Synthesizing the evidence up to 2026, several themes emerge regarding methods for 1–5-day index and FX futures return forecasting, especially when judged by out-of-sample performance net of costs.

First, gradient boosting and related ensemble methods over carefully engineered feature sets, particularly those incorporating sentiment and cross-asset information, provide the strongest documented performance on short-horizon FX and Treasury futures, as exemplified by the macro news sentiment case study. In that work, FinBERT-derived global news sentiment indices feed into an XGBoost classifier that predicts next-day returns for EUR/USD, USD/JPY, and 10-year U.S. Treasury futures, delivering cost-adjusted Sharpe ratios well above conventional benchmarks and CAGRs exceeding 50% in FX and over 22% in bonds. This combination of rigorous expanding-window evaluation, purged cross-validation, and explicit transaction-cost deduction constitutes a gold standard for demonstrating genuine forecasting skill. Analogous approaches using commodity sentiment and gradient boosting for S&P 500 index futures also show substantial economic gains, though cost treatment is less clearly documented.  

Second, specialized deep-learning architectures—such as LSTMs, CNNs, and hybrid combinations like VSN+LSTM and xLSTM—have proven effective for daily futures prediction when evaluated with unified, large-scale protocols. The deep CNN model for six futures from the Chicago Mercantile Exchange and New York Mercantile Exchange achieves significantly higher average annual return and Sharpe ratio than previous nearest neighbour, SVM, and deep feed-forward networks, with improvements of 6.1–53.0% in average annual return and 53.0–199.0% in Sharpe ratio. The large-scale benchmark of modern deep-learning architectures for financial time series further shows that hybrid models like VSN+LSTM and xLSTM achieve the highest overall Sharpe ratios and the largest breakeven transaction-cost buffers, indicating robustness to trading frictions across 2010–2025 daily futures and FX data. These findings suggest that, for 1–5-day horizons, deep sequence models optimized for risk-adjusted performance are powerful tools when coupled with rigorous backtesting and cost-aware evaluation.  

Third, TSFMs such as Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, and Moirai-2, while technologically impressive, have not yet demonstrated compelling net-of-cost out-of-sample alpha on daily equity returns and, by extension, on index and FX futures in the published literature. The equity-return benchmarking study finds that TSFMs dominate model rankings but deliver only sparse, small gains over random-walk baselines, with statistically significant improvements limited to a few tasks. Comparative analyses emphasize that off-the-shelf TSFMs often underperform on financial data, that finance-native pretraining and few-shot fine-tuning are needed to approach strong supervised models, and that benchmark contamination can inflate apparent accuracy. TSFMs do improve LLM-based trading decisions and perform well within text-augmented systems, but their standalone value as primary engines of short-horizon alpha remains constrained by low signal-to-noise ratios and theoretical predictability limits.  

Fourth, constituent-to-index and cross-series forecasting approaches appear promising, especially when constituents have clear economic linkages to the target index or futures. Multivariate Chronos-2 modelling across equities and rates shows that joint inference across related series improves statistical accuracy, while less related series can degrade performance. Commodity sentiment models for S&P 500 index futures and arbitrage indices for CSI 300 spot–futures relationships demonstrate that modelling economically relevant constituents—commodities and spot indices, respectively—can yield substantial economic gains. Macro news sentiment models for FX and Treasury futures further illustrate that exogenous sentiment indices can function as powerful per-asset constituents, driving high Sharpe and robust CAGRs. However, in Brazil, constituent-based frameworks are mostly applied at monthly horizons for Ibovespa, and there is a lack of published 1–5-day constituent-to-index studies on WIN or WDO futures.  

Finally, evaluation protocols and transaction-cost treatment are decisive in assessing what truly works. Studies that lack walk-forward or purged evaluation, rely on static splits, and ignore transaction costs—such as some Ibovespa and gold price forecasting papers—can show high \(R^2\) and hit rates but provide limited guidance on real-world trading viability. In contrast, research that employs rolling-origin schemes, expanding-window cross-validation, unified backtesting frameworks, and cost-adjusted Sharpe ratios offers credible evidence of tradable forecasting skill.  

In practical terms, for an academic or practitioner seeking to forecast 1–5-day index and FX futures returns, the current evidence suggests the following priorities. One should begin with robust supervised learning methods—gradient boosting and deep sequence models—trained on rich feature sets that include technical indicators, cross-asset signals, and sentiment indices, evaluated under walk-forward protocols with explicit transaction-cost deductions. TSFMs can be employed as priors or auxiliary models, especially for multivariate and covariate-informed tasks, but should not be relied upon as sole engines of alpha at daily horizons without careful fine-tuning and benchmark validation. Constituent-based models should be explored in markets where economically linked assets—commodities, FX pairs, interest-rate futures—are available and likely to influence index or FX futures, as they have shown promise in the S&P 500 and CSI 300 contexts. In Brazilian markets, there remains a substantial opportunity for future research to bring these methodologies and evaluation standards to WIN and WDO, bridging the gap between proprietary trading practice and published academic evidence.
