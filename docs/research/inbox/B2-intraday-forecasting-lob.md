# Deep Learning and Gradient-Boosting Methods for Short-Horizon Limit Order Book Forecasting: A 2026 Survey

The forecasting of one-to-five-minute price movements from limit order book (LOB) and trade data has evolved into a rich methodological ecosystem that spans convolutional–recurrent neural networks, transformer architectures, foundation models, and carefully engineered gradient-boosted decision trees. Over roughly the past decade, a sequence of benchmark datasets and models—beginning with FI‑2010 and DeepLOB, and extending through HLOB, LOBFrame, TLOB, LiT, LOBERT, and the 2026 inference–compute frontier study—have progressively clarified both the promise and the limitations of machine learning for high-frequency price prediction. These works show that modest but statistically significant predictive power exists at horizons corresponding to tens of LOB updates, especially for large-tick, moderately liquid equities, yet they also demonstrate that translating classification accuracy into net-profitable trading signals after realistic transaction costs is far more difficult than early results suggested. At the same time, comparative studies and crypto-LOB experiments reveal that carefully constructed order-flow features fed into gradient-boosted trees can match or surpass deep architectures on some short-horizon tasks, raising questions about when added depth and architectural complexity are truly warranted. As of 2026, the empirical picture is that deep models dominate public leaderboards on canonical equity LOB datasets, but their advantage over strong tree-based baselines shrinks once microstructural heterogeneity, label design, and cost-aware evaluation are correctly handled, and robust evidence of transfer to index or FX futures with single tick sizes and deep books remains scarce.

## Limit Order Books, Forecasting Horizons, and Benchmark Datasets

### The limit order book as a forecasting substrate

Electronic limit order markets match buy and sell orders through a continuously updated book of resting limit orders, organized into bid and ask queues at discrete price levels. A typical LOB snapshot records, at each level on the bid and ask sides, the quoted price and the aggregate queue volume, with the best bid and best ask forming the inside market and their average defining the mid-price. Bids represent quantities that buyers are willing to pay at or below specified prices, while asks represent quantities sellers are willing to accept at or above specified prices, and deeper levels reveal queued liquidity at less favorable prices that collectively encode the supply–demand configuration of the market at a given instant. Short-horizon mid-price movements are thus driven by imbalances between the buying and selling pressure embedded in this multi-level structure and the arrival of market orders that consume resting liquidity, which makes LOB and trade data an attractive substrate for machine learning models that seek to forecast price changes over the next few seconds to minutes.

In practice, the LOB can be represented either in its raw form—price and volume at the top \(k\) levels on each side—or through engineered features such as order flow imbalance (OFI), microprice, volume clocks, and various volatility and momentum indicators. For example, Kercheval and Zhang constructed weighted \(k\)-th level mid-prices and quantity imbalances from LOB snapshots and applied linear models, including logistic regression, to classify the direction of the next mid-price movement, illustrating the predictive value of carefully engineered order-flow features even before the deep learning era. Microprice-based features incorporate the idea that the “true” price is closer to the side with more liquidity, while volume clocks rescale time according to traded or queued volume, thereby capturing the uneven pace of market activity; these representations have been explored in a variety of financial microstructure studies, though the models surveyed in this report predominantly use raw levels or simple derived imbalances. The choice between raw and engineered features has deep implications for both model complexity and interpretability: raw input deep networks must implicitly learn non-linear transformations akin to OFI or microprice, whereas tree-based models thrive when such structure is made explicit.

### Short-horizon forecasting in event and clock time

In the deep LOB forecasting literature, horizons are often specified in terms of a fixed number of LOB updates or messages rather than empirical clock time, with 10, 20, 50, or 100 updates serving as common benchmarks. For FI‑2010, which aggregates order book events over a relatively illiquid Nasdaq Nordic market, 10 updates correspond roughly to sub-second scales, while 50 and 100 updates can reach into the one-to-five-minute range, though the precise mapping depends on the intraday activity of each stock. DeepLOB, for instance, predicts the direction of mid-price movements over horizons ranging from 10 to 100 events, while HLOB systematically analyzes informational degradation across the same horizons, linking them to the microstructural properties of the underlying stocks, such as tick size and liquidity. Later works like TLOB adopt a similar event-based horizon design but explicitly connect these horizons to average spreads and transaction-cost scales when constructing labels for price trend classification.

Some more recent studies, particularly in cryptocurrency markets, define horizons in clock time, sampling LOB snapshots at fixed intervals, such as 100 milliseconds, 500 milliseconds, or multiple seconds, and then predicting mid-price movements over these fixed clock horizons. In their study of BTC/USDT limit order books from Bybit, the authors benchmark models on LOB snapshots sampled at 100 ms to multi-second intervals, evaluating both binary up/down and ternary up/flat/down labeling schemes, which directly correspond to one-to-five-minute ranges when multiple steps are considered. This clock-time framing aligns more naturally with practitioners’ notion of “one-to-five-minute price forecasting” and makes transaction-cost considerations more straightforward, though it introduces additional variability due to the heterogeneity of event arrival rates, which event-based labels implicitly normalize.

### FI‑2010 and equity LOB benchmarks

The FI‑2010 dataset remains the canonical public benchmark for LOB-based mid-price prediction tasks, and nearly all deep LOB architectures reviewed here use it for at least part of their evaluation. Introduced in Ntakaris et al.’s “Benchmark dataset for mid-price forecasting of limit order book data with machine learning methods,” FI‑2010 comprises ten consecutive days of downsampled, pre-normalised limit order book data from five stocks traded on the Nasdaq Nordic stock market, with each LOB snapshot capturing 10 levels on each side, yielding 40 raw features per time step. In addition to these raw features, the benchmark paper defines an extended 144-feature representation that includes various engineered indicators, such as derivatives of mid-price, spreads, and volatility, and provides baseline results for linear and non-linear models under several labeling schemes. DeepLOB and many successors build on the 40-feature representation and the up/neutral/down mid-price movement labels at multiple horizons proposed in this benchmark, which is why FI‑2010 continues to anchor comparisons despite its limited temporal coverage and the relatively illiquid nature of the underlying stocks.

Several later works highlight limitations of FI‑2010 and stress the importance of careful reporting of feature conventions, horizon labels, and class balance when using it. The 2026 inference–compute frontier study explicitly notes that FI‑2010’s feature conventions, horizon labels, class balance, and reproduced rankings require careful treatment, pointing to prior discrepancies in how researchers constructed labels and split train and test sets. Briola, Bartolucci, and Aste similarly argue that traditional machine learning metrics applied to FI‑2010 can paint an overly optimistic picture of forecasting quality, and that microstructural differences across stocks and time periods can materially affect performance, creating challenges for transferability and robustness assessments. Despite these caveats, FI‑2010 remains indispensable because it is one of the few publicly available equity LOB datasets with standardized labels and enough historical depth to support large-scale deep learning experiments, and it is tightly integrated into the LOBFrame codebase that underpins many recent studies.

Beyond FI‑2010, DeepLOB introduced a second dataset consisting of one year of LOB data from the London Stock Exchange (LSE), encompassing multiple instruments and offering a more realistic setting for out-of-sample evaluations. While this LSE dataset is not as widely shared or standardized as FI‑2010, the DeepLOB paper reports that the model delivers stable out-of-sample prediction accuracy across a variety of instruments, suggesting that high-frequency LOB patterns exhibit some degree of stationarity within a given equity market. More recent work from the UCL Financial Computing group focuses on three real-world LOB datasets, each including fifteen stocks traded on NASDAQ, which are used to evaluate the HLOB architecture and to study information persistence across small-, medium-, and large-tick stocks. Collectively, these equity datasets anchor the empirical evidence on deep learning models and provide the principal basis for the comparisons discussed in this survey.

### Cryptocurrency LOB datasets

Cryptocurrency markets offer a complementary testbed for LOB forecasting models, characterized by continuous trading, often narrower tick sizes, and different liquidity and volatility regimes compared with traditional equities. The Bybit BTC/USDT dataset used in “Exploring Microstructural Dynamics in Cryptocurrency Limit Order Books: Better Inputs Matter More Than Stacking Another Hidden Layer” consists of 100 ms LOB snapshots over extended periods, with multiple depth levels capturing bid and ask queues. The authors analyze this dataset using a range of models—from logistic regression and XGBoost to deep architectures like DeepLOB and Conv1D+LSTM—and investigate the impact of data filtering pipelines (Kalman and Savitzky–Golay smoothing) and labeling schemes (binary and ternary) on performance. Separately, the TLOB paper introduces a Bitcoin dataset alongside FI‑2010 and NASDAQ equities, using it to demonstrate that their transformer-based architecture and the simpler MLPLOB model maintain or improve performance across differing market conditions and assets. These crypto datasets provide the principal empirical basis for assessing cross-asset transfer within the published LOB deep learning literature, although they still leave index and FX futures largely unexplored.

### Message-level data and foundation models

A distinct strand of recent research models LOB dynamics at the message level rather than at the snapshot level, capturing the irregular timing and event-by-event structure of order flow. LOBERT, a generative AI foundation model for limit order book messages, treats complete multi-dimensional messages as tokens and learns a “grammar” of market microstructure through unsupervised pretraining, before fine-tuning on downstream tasks such as mid-price movement prediction or next-message forecasting. This approach relies on a novel tokenization scheme and a multi-modal embedding layer that incorporates discrete message types and continuous price, volume, and time information, enabling the model to exploit the hybrid discrete–continuous nature of LOB data. While LOBERT moves beyond traditional snapshot-based representations and has the potential to unify multiple tasks and horizons, its published experiments focus on equity markets, and the connection of its message-level representation to classic snapshots and engineered features like OFI and microprice is still an active area of investigation.

## DeepLOB, HLOB, and the LOBFrame Ecosystem

### DeepLOB: CNN–LSTM modeling of LOB snapshots

DeepLOB, introduced by Zhang, Zohren, and Roberts, was the first large-scale deep learning model specifically designed to predict short-term price movements from LOB data. Its architecture combines convolutional filters that operate along the spatial structure of the limit order book with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) modules that capture longer time dependencies, yielding a hybrid system that processes LOB snapshots as sequences and extracts hierarchical features analogous to those used in computer vision. The convolutional layers, including an Inception-style module, are applied to matrices representing price and volume across multiple levels on both sides of the book, capturing local patterns such as depth imbalances near the best bid and ask, while the LSTMs process sequences of these feature maps over time, learning how patterns evolve in response to incoming order flow.

DeepLOB uses FI‑2010 as its primary benchmark, training on 10 days of pre-normalised data from five Nasdaq Nordic stocks, with input sequences consisting of the last \(T\) LOB snapshots and labels corresponding to the direction of mid-price changes over horizons of 10, 20, and 50 events. Each snapshot encodes the top 10 bid and ask levels with their prices and volumes, producing a 40-dimensional raw feature vector per time step, which is stacked over time to form a \(T \times 40\) input matrix. The label design follows the benchmark dataset: three classes—up, neutral, and down—are defined by comparing the future mid-price at horizon \(h\) to the current mid-price, with thresholds set in multiples of the tick size or small absolute price changes to avoid classification driven by noise. This k-step mid-price change labeling scheme aims to capture directional predictability at short horizons, and DeepLOB’s original results showed that its CNN–LSTM architecture significantly outperformed logistic regression, support vector machines, and other conventional baseline models on FI‑2010.

Performance metrics reported in the DeepLOB paper include accuracy and F1 scores, with the model achieving state-of-the-art performance on FI‑2010 relative to the benchmark baselines. For example, DeepLOB attained remarkably high classification accuracy on the up/neutral/down task across multiple horizons, beating all existing algorithms evaluated on FI‑2010 at that time, which prompted considerable interest in deep LOB modeling. The authors further tested the model on a proprietary LSE dataset consisting of one year of limit order data for multiple instruments, reporting stable out-of-sample accuracy, though detailed comparisons with logistic or naive baselines are less extensively documented for this second dataset. Importantly, many subsequent works treat DeepLOB as a reference point rather than an upper bound, and newer architectures like HLOB are explicitly designed to overcome structural limitations of DeepLOB’s convolutional design. As for compute, the original DeepLOB paper does not report GPU-hours per training run; given the relatively small size of FI‑2010 and the moderate depth of the CNN–LSTM, independent reimplementations suggest that training DeepLOB on FI‑2010 completes within a few hours on a single modern GPU, while larger datasets like LSE require correspondingly more compute.

### LOBFrame: a standardized pipeline for LOB forecasting

LOBFrame, released by Briola, Bartolucci, and Aste, is a model-agnostic open-source framework designed to standardize preprocessing, modeling, and evaluation for deep learning on large-scale LOB data. Built around insights from previous research on LOB dynamics, it integrates data transformation and cleaning, ultra-fast training, validation, and testing steps, and trading simulation modules that evaluate the practical quality of a model’s outputs. LOBFrame’s strength lies in its comprehensive nature: it supports multiple datasets, including FI‑2010 and three NASDAQ equity LOB datasets used in HLOB and Deep limit order book forecasting, and accommodates a range of models, from traditional machine learning algorithms to CNN–LSTM architectures like DeepLOB and newer designs such as HLOB.

The Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting paper uses LOBFrame to explore the predictability of high-frequency mid-price changes for a heterogeneous set of NASDAQ stocks and to release a robust code base for future empirical work. In this study, the authors exploit state-of-the-art deep learning methodologies, primarily using DeepLOB as a case study, and argue that microstructural characteristics—such as tick size, liquidity, and intraday volatility—strongly influence the efficacy of deep learning methods. Their results show that high forecasting power in terms of conventional metrics like accuracy or F1 does not necessarily correspond to actionable trading signals once transaction costs and execution uncertainty are accounted for, prompting them to propose an operational framework that evaluates predictions by focusing on the probability of accurately forecasting complete transactions, including both entry and exit. This framework provides an avenue for academics and practitioners to make informed decisions about where deep learning techniques are effective and where they fail, and it is implemented directly in LOBFrame as part of the evaluation pipeline.

LOBFrame processes raw limit order book data into standardized representations suitable for both deep and shallow models, including sequences of LOB snapshots over fixed horizons and derived features aligned with microstructural research. While many integrated models use only raw price and volume levels, the framework is flexible enough to incorporate OFI-like features, microprice, and volume clocks, thereby bridging the gap between traditional feature-engineering approaches and end-to-end deep architectures. For the datasets considered—three NASDAQ datasets each including 15 stocks—LOBFrame enables large-scale experiments with hundreds of millions of LOB updates, implying substantial training compute for deep architectures like HLOB, although explicit GPU-hour figures are again not reported. Nevertheless, by releasing code and data preprocessing recipes, LOBFrame greatly improves reproducibility relative to the early DeepLOB era, where proprietary data and ad hoc preprocessing made replication challenging.

### HLOB: modeling information persistence and higher-order spatial structure

HLOB (“Homological Limit Order Book”) is a large-scale deep learning architecture developed within the LOBFrame ecosystem to forecast mid-price changes and to study information persistence and structure in limit order books. Its key novelty lies in the exploitation of an Information Filtering Network, the Triangulated Maximally Filtered Graph (TMFG), to unveil deeper and non-trivial dependency structures among volume levels, combined with inspiration from Homological Convolutional Neural Networks to impose deterministic design choices on the architecture. Specifically, the authors compute mutual information between all pairs of volume levels across the LOB and use the TMFG algorithm to construct a planar graph that preserves higher-order structures up to four-variable cliques, effectively distilling the essential dependency structure from the full correlation matrix. This process transforms the spatial information in the LOB into simplicial complexes—edges, triangles, and tetrads—that capture relationships of different order among volume and price levels, which HLOB then leverages to superimpose a spatial structure on the deep network’s convolutional filters.

HLOB is tested against nine state-of-the-art deep learning alternatives, including DeepLOB, on three real-world NASDAQ LOB datasets, each covering 15 stocks categorized by tick size (small, medium, large), and uses mid-price change direction forecasting at high-frequency resolutions as its primary task. The label design mirrors prior work: mid-price direction (up, down, neutral) over horizons of 10, 50, and 100 LOB updates, with thresholds chosen to ensure that small noise-level movements do not dominate classification outcomes. One of the main contributions of HLOB is to show that modeling higher-order and non-trivial dependency structures among LOB volume levels yields performance gains over traditional CNN–LSTM models like DeepLOB, particularly at short horizons and for large-tick stocks where the spatial configuration of the book carries persistent information. Empirically, HLOB outperforms alternative architectures in roughly 73.3% of tested scenarios when predicting 10 updates ahead, maintains a strong advantage at 50 updates (60% outperformance), especially for large-tick assets, and sees its edge diminish at 100 updates (33% of scenarios), consistent with the degradation of spatial information over longer horizons.

These results highlight two microstructural aspects captured by HLOB: first, that the LOB possesses an underlying spatial structure requiring modeling of higher-order dependencies among volume and price levels; second, that the emergence and persistence of these dependency structures depend on tick size and vary across prediction horizons. While HLOB’s architecture is more complex than DeepLOB, the authors stress that the model’s design is deterministic, grounded in information-filtering and topological principles, which helps avoid arbitrary architectural choices and enhances interpretability. In terms of input representation, HLOB operates on raw volume and price levels transformed via TMFG-derived spatial graphs, rather than explicitly engineered OFI or microprice features, relying on its convolutional structure to learn relevant aggregations. Training HLOB on large-scale NASDAQ datasets within LOBFrame implies significant compute—likely tens of GPU-hours per full experiment—but as with other deep LOB works, the paper does not specify detailed compute budgets, reflecting a broader gap in reporting practices.

### Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting: microstructural guide and operational evaluation

The Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting paper serves both as a methodological contribution and as a microstructural guide to interpreting LOB deep learning results. The authors exploit cutting-edge deep learning methods, including DeepLOB, to explore the predictability of high-frequency mid-price changes for a heterogeneous set of NASDAQ stocks and to quantify how stock-specific microstructural characteristics influence forecasting efficacy. Their results are twofold: first, they demonstrate that deep learning models can attain high predictive performance on certain stocks and horizons, but that this performance varies systematically with tick size, liquidity, and volatility; second, they argue that standard classification metrics like accuracy and F1 score fail to adequately assess forecast quality in the LOB context.

To address this, they propose an operational framework that evaluates predictions’ practicality by focusing on the probability of accurately forecasting complete transactions, which includes entry at the bid or ask and exit at a future price that justifies paying transaction costs. By linking stocks’ “predictability rate”—the probability of correctly forecasting the direction of mid-price movements over a given horizon—directly to microstructural properties, the framework allows practitioners to evaluate model effectiveness beyond raw classification metrics and to assess whether forecasts can be converted into profitable trades after spreads and fees. This reinforces the insight that label design must match economic objectives: labels that simply classify small directional moves may produce attractive F1 scores but correspond to trades that cannot overcome realistic transaction costs, especially when costs are on the order of two ticks per side, as is common in many equity and futures markets.

Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting is tightly integrated with LOBFrame, which is designed to be model-agnostic and to facilitate the systematic study and evaluation of LOB forecasting models across multiple datasets and architectures. The framework supports reproducible experiments with DeepLOB, HLOB, and other architectures, and its standardized pipeline for data transformation, training, validation, and trading simulation helps to clarify which results are robust and which are sensitive to label conventions, splits, and microstructural differences. This contribution is central to contemporary understanding of LOB forecasting because it explicitly confronts the gap between algorithmic performance and trading profitability, providing an empirical basis for later questions in this survey about label designs that survive transaction costs and about failures of early results to replicate under more realistic evaluation schemes.

## Transformer Architectures: TLOB, LiT, and LOBERT

### TLOB and MLPLOB: dual-attention transformers and simple MLP baselines

Price Trend Prediction (PTP) based on LOB data faces challenges of robustness across market conditions and assets, and TLOB addresses these by adopting a dual-attention transformer architecture designed specifically for LOB snapshots. The TLOB paper introduces two models: MLPLOB, a simple multilayer perceptron adapted to LOB data, and TLOB, a transformer-based approach that uses dual attention mechanisms to capture spatial and temporal dependencies in LOB sequences. Both models take as input a sequence of LOB time series consisting of the last \(T\) LOB snapshots for the top 10 LOB levels, with each snapshot encoding price and volume features for bids and asks. The dual-attention blocks in TLOB comprise self-attention over LOB snapshots along the temporal axis to capture time-wise dependencies among consecutive snapshots and self-attention over LOB features along the spatial axis to capture relationships among different price–volume features within a snapshot.

This design allows TLOB to adaptively focus on microstructural patterns relevant to both short and long horizons, making it particularly effective for longer-horizon predictions and volatile market conditions. The authors conduct comprehensive evaluations of MLPLOB and TLOB on the benchmark FI‑2010 dataset, on NASDAQ stocks (Tesla and Intel) using LOBSTER-style data, and on a recent Bitcoin dataset, comparing their models against state-of-the-art baselines. Across four horizons, TLOB and MLPLOB surpass state-of-the-art performances on every dataset, with TLOB performing best on larger horizons and MLPLOB performing best on shorter ones. On FI‑2010, TLOB exceeds the state of the art by an average of 3.7 F1-score points across horizons, while on Tesla and Intel, it delivers average improvements of 1.3 and 7.7 in F1-score, respectively, relative to prior deep learning models. On the Bitcoin dataset, TLOB outperforms state-of-the-art performance by an average of 1.1 in F1-score.

A central contribution of TLOB is its new labeling method, designed to remove horizon bias and to reflect transaction costs more realistically. Instead of defining trends solely by the mid-price at a fixed horizon, the authors experiment with thresholds based on the average spread, which is a primary transaction cost, thereby ensuring that labeled trends correspond to price moves that could feasibly cover costs. They show empirically that defining trend thresholds based on average spread significantly impacts model performance and profitability, with performance deterioration observed when thresholds are set to reflect realistic execution costs rather than small price changes. This finding reinforces the Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting insight that high F1 scores on naive labels do not necessarily translate into profitable strategies and emphasizes the importance of label design aligned with transaction-cost constraints.

In terms of input representation, both MLPLOB and TLOB primarily use raw LOB snapshots—price and volume at the top 10 levels—stacked over time, rather than engineered OFI or microprice features. Their success in surpassing prior deep architectures suggests that transformers can learn complex spatial–temporal relationships directly from raw inputs when provided with sufficient data and regularization, though the strong performance of the simple MLPLOB baseline also indicates that additional depth is not always necessary. The TLOB repository (`https://github.com/LeonardoBerti00/TLOB`) provides public code for both models and experimental protocols, improving reproducibility and making approximate compute costs transparent to users; while the paper does not state GPU-hours per training run, the moderate size of FI‑2010 and the Tesla/Intel data implies that training TLOB and MLPLOB can be accomplished on commodity GPUs within hours.

Beyond performance, TLOB’s authors note a broader phenomenon: stock price predictability has declined over time, with an empirical drop of about 6.68 in F1-score across years, highlighting growing market efficiency. This temporal decline in predictability suggests that deep models tuned on older datasets may overstate the achievable performance on contemporary markets and underscores the necessity of continuous benchmarking and reevaluation, especially as transaction-cost structures and market participants evolve. TLOB thus contributes both architectural advances and an important cautionary note about the changing nature of predictability in LOB-driven markets.

### LiT: Limit Order Book Transformer with structured patches

LiT (Limit Order Book Transformer) introduces a transformer-based architecture for short-term market movement forecasting using high-frequency LOB data, explicitly eliminating the need for convolutional layers that dominated prior approaches. Inspired by the success of transformer models in natural language processing and computer vision, LiT captures LOB features using structured patches and processes them through self-attention layers, followed by LSTM layers to enhance temporal modeling. The model architecture consists of three main components: a linear projection concatenated with positional embeddings to represent structured patches from LOB data efficiently; transformer layers utilizing self-attention mechanisms to encode spatial and temporal dependencies between patches; and LSTM layers to further model long-term temporal dependencies. This design blends patch-based vision-style representation with the sequential modeling capabilities of LSTMs, aiming to capture both microstructural patterns within a single snapshot and their evolution over time.

LiT is evaluated on multiple LOB datasets across different prediction horizons, including FI‑2010 and other high-frequency equity LOB datasets, and consistently outperforms traditional machine learning methods and state-of-the-art deep learning baselines, particularly CNN-based architectures like DeepLOB. Its input representation involves partitioning the LOB snapshot matrix—comprised of price and volume levels—into patches that serve as tokens for the transformer, analogously to how image patches are used in vision transformers. This allows the self-attention mechanism to model interactions among local regions of the book (e.g., near-best levels versus deeper levels) and to learn non-local dependencies that convolutional filters might miss. LiT’s label design adheres to standard mid-price change classification, using k-step horizons and thresholds similar to those in FI‑2010 and related datasets, and its reported performance indicates that attention-based architectures can exploit LOB structure more effectively than convolutional models at equivalent compute budgets.

The LiT paper does not extensively discuss transaction-cost-aware evaluation or provide detailed GPU-hour training costs, focusing instead on comparative forecasting performance across horizons and datasets. Nevertheless, its contribution lies in demonstrating that patch-based transformer architectures can be competitive with and even superior to CNN–LSTM models for LOB forecasting, reinforcing the trend toward transformer adoption seen in TLOB. Given that LiT avoids convolutions and uses structured patches, it may offer advantages in terms of parameter efficiency and flexibility in handling different LOB depths and asset classes, though empirical evidence on transfer to non-equity markets remains limited.

### LOBERT: a foundation model for message-level LOB data

LOBERT (“Limit Order Book BERT”) represents a significant shift from snapshot-based to message-level modeling in LOB forecasting. The authors introduce LOBERT as a general-purpose encoder-only foundation model for LOB data, suitable for downstream fine-tuning on tasks such as predicting mid-price movements and next messages. To adapt the original BERT architecture to LOB data, LOBERT uses a novel tokenization scheme that treats complete multi-dimensional messages—each containing information such as event type (e.g., limit order addition, cancellation, market order), price, volume, and timestamp—as single tokens, while retaining continuous representations for price differences, volume values, and time. A multi-modal embedding layer merges representations from four sources: discrete message tokens, continuous price differences, continuous volume values, and, optionally, LOB snapshot representations integrated through a learned gating mechanism.

LOBERT’s training methodology involves a two-phase strategy designed to first build a robust understanding of the “language” of the LOB message stream and then to refine this understanding for predictive tasks. In the first stage, the model is pre-trained on large-scale message data using masked token prediction and other self-supervised objectives, learning generic representations of market microstructure dynamics. In the second stage, the pretrained model is fine-tuned with task-specific heads to predict mid-price movements over arbitrary horizons or to forecast the next message given the current message sequence. This foundation model approach mirrors developments in NLP, where large pretrained encoders are adapted to diverse tasks, and positions LOBERT as a flexible building block for various forecasting and simulation problems in market microstructure.

Experimental results show that LOBERT achieves leading performance in tasks such as mid-price movement prediction and next-message forecasting, while reducing the required context length compared to previous methods that relied on cumbersome data representations. By modeling messages directly, LOBERT can, in principle, capture fine-grained patterns of order flow and cancellation that are smeared out in snapshot-based representations, potentially enabling more accurate short-horizon prediction and richer downstream analyses. In terms of input representation, LOBERT operates at a different level of abstraction: it does not use engineered OFI or microprice features as inputs, but its multi-modal embeddings implicitly encode order flow imbalance and price dynamics over sequences of messages. The label designs in LOBERT’s experiments follow standard mid-price movement classification, but the model’s architecture is compatible with more complex labeling schemes, including triple-barrier labels or path-dependent profit-and-loss measures, though such applications have not yet been extensively documented in the published literature.

LOBERT’s codebase is not fully visible in the provided excerpts, but the AlphaXiv entry notes its contribution and situates it within Juho Kanniainen’s broader work on LOB forecasting, which includes the benchmark FI‑2010 dataset. As a foundation model, LOBERT likely requires substantial compute for pretraining—potentially hundreds of GPU-hours on large message datasets—yet detailed training budgets are not reported in the arXiv summaries. Its main impact on the present survey lies in illustrating the move toward general-purpose LOB encoders and in highlighting that message-level modeling can extend the scope of LOB forecasting beyond simple snapshot-based mid-price movement classification.

## The Inference–Compute Frontier and Crypto LOB Comparisons

### The inference–compute frontier and FastBiNLOB

The 2026 study “The Inference-Compute Frontier and a Latency-Efficient Architecture for Limit Order Book Prediction” asks whether a scaling-law-style inference–compute frontier appears in LOB prediction. Using FI‑2010 and a suite of models ranging from small decision trees to neural LOB architectures, the authors analyze the realized empirical frontier of predictive loss versus structural forward work, finding that it is well summarized by a power law. Specifically, they construct a heterogeneous frontier on raw LOB40 features using decision trees, histogram gradient boosting, CatBoost symmetric trees, and random-convolution logistic models, and show that this frontier extrapolates across multiple orders of magnitude and attains an \(R^{2} = 0.941\) on excluded MLPLOB target frontier points when MLPLOB operating points are omitted from the fit. This suggests that structural forward work—measured as the computational effort per inference—is a useful empirical capacity coordinate for heterogeneous LOB predictors on FI‑2010.

The study operates in two experimental lanes, both using FI‑2010. In the deployment lane, it uses the full 144 FI‑2010 feature set with train days 1–7 and test days 8–10, matching the MLPLOB/TLOB-style comparison protocol with macro-F1 and batch-one latency as principal metrics. A wide range of model configurations are considered: nine decision-tree models, 30 histogram-gradient-boosting models, six CatBoost models, and eleven random-convolution configurations, including an additional six small W10 bridge models. Structural work and measured latency are reported for each configuration; for instance, histogram gradient boosting has a lower median structural work of 528 units but a much higher median latency of 4.34 ms than some alternatives, highlighting the tradeoff between computational work and serving latency. By fitting a frontier across these models and comparing it to deep architectures like MLPLOB and FastBiNLOB, the authors demonstrate that measured latency is a separate design target and that efficient architectures can preserve useful computation while reducing serving latency.

FastBiNLOB, introduced in this study, is a latency-efficient architecture that aims to sit near the inference–compute frontier while achieving competitive predictive performance. It combines elements of binary classification, convolutional processing, and structured feature engineering to reduce the structural work needed for inference, thereby enabling faster prediction at scale without sacrificing much accuracy. While details of its input representation and label design are not fully elaborated in the excerpt, FastBiNLOB’s evaluation on FI‑2010 under the MLPLOB/TLOB protocol suggests that it uses standard LOB40 or 144-feature inputs and mid-price movement labels across short horizons. The study’s central insight is that deep architectures and tree-based models occupy comparable positions along a power-law frontier of predictive loss versus compute, implying that complexity should be justified not only by accuracy gains but also by latency and deployment constraints. For practitioners concerned with one-to-five-minute forecasting in real-time systems, this frontier provides a guide to balancing model choice, compute budget, and latency targets.

### Crypto LOBs: better inputs versus deeper networks

In “Exploring Microstructural Dynamics in Cryptocurrency Limit Order Books: Better Inputs Matter More Than Stacking Another Hidden Layer,” the authors investigate BTC/USDT LOB dynamics on Bybit and compare a spectrum of models, from interpretable baselines like logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees to deep architectures such as DeepLOB and Conv1D+LSTM. The dataset consists of LOB snapshots sampled at 100 ms to multi-second intervals, capturing multiple depth levels for bids and asks, and omitting macro and cross-asset inputs to focus purely on microstructural supply–demand imbalances. The authors introduce two data-filtering pipelines—Kalman and Savitzky–Golay smoothing—to reduce noise and emphasize signal in the LOB time series, and evaluate both binary labels (up/down mid-price movement) and ternary labels (up/flat/down), which map naturally onto short-horizon forecasting tasks.

Their results reveal that, with data preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning, simpler models like logistic regression and gradient-boosted decision trees can match and even exceed the performance of more complex deep networks, offering faster inference and greater interpretability. In particular, training CatBoost (a gradient-boosted decision tree variant with ordered boosting) yielded accuracies of up to 63% on short-term movement prediction, depending on feature informativity, which is comparable to or better than some deep baselines. The models use both baseline inputs—top \(k\) bid and ask price and volume levels, bid–ask spread, recent mid-price returns—and engineered metrics like rolling window volatility, order flow imbalance across multiple depth levels, and principal component summaries of the LOB snapshot. This feature-rich representation allows tree-based models to exploit complex non-linear relationships without the need for deep architectures, and the authors argue that improved inputs matter more than stacking additional hidden layers in this cryptocurrency LOB setting.

Importantly, the crypto LOB study reinforces conclusions drawn from equity datasets about the role of microstructural heterogeneity and label design. For BTC/USDT, where tick size and liquidity characteristics differ markedly from Nasdaq equities, the efficacy of deep models versus tree-based models depends critically on how LOB snapshots are filtered, how labels are constructed, and which features are included. The fact that gradient-boosted trees can compete with deep networks when high-quality order-flow features and smoothing are applied provides direct head-to-head evidence that depth is not universally required for short-horizon LOB forecasting, especially when models are constrained by latency or interpretability considerations. This study thus contributes directly to the question of whether gradient-boosted trees over order-flow features can match deep models at one-to-five-minute horizons, at least in crypto markets, and complements FastBiNLOB’s evidence on FI‑2010.

## Classical and Gradient-Boosted Models Over Order-Flow Features

### Early logistic regression and linear models

Before the deep learning era, LOB forecasting relied heavily on logistic regression and other linear or kernel-based models applied to handcrafted features that summarized order book and trade dynamics. Kercheval and Zhang’s work, for instance, demonstrated that features such as weighted \(k\)-th level mid-prices, quantity imbalances between bid and ask sides, and short-horizon returns extracted from LOB snapshots could produce useful signals when fed into linear models for next-mid-price movement classification. These models operated on equity LOB data, capturing the intuition that depth imbalances at the top levels often precede directional moves over very short horizons, and they served as baselines for subsequent assessments of deep models. Ntakaris et al., in releasing FI‑2010, provided logistic regression and other baseline metrics under standard labeling schemes, further solidifying logistic models as reference points for evaluating new architectures.

In FI‑2010, logistic regression applied to the 144-feature extended representation (which includes derived mid-price, spread, volatility, and momentum features) achieved moderate accuracy and F1 scores, but was significantly outperformed by non-linear models and, later, deep architectures like DeepLOB. This performance gap motivated the adoption of CNN–LSTM models and, subsequently, transformer architectures, yet it also underscored that much of the predictive power resides in microstructural features themselves; deep models excel largely because they can automatically infer non-linear combinations of order-flow features that earlier studies handcrafted. Logistic regression remains relevant as a naive baseline: it represents the performance level achievable by simple linear classification on standard order-flow features, and thus serves as a lower bound in most deep LOB studies.

### Gradient-boosted trees and comparison to deep networks

Gradient-boosted decision trees, including XGBoost and CatBoost, offer a non-linear alternative to linear models by fitting ensembles of regression trees through gradient-based loss minimization. In LOB applications, these models typically operate on flattened sequences of order-flow features, integrating multiple time steps and depth levels into a single high-dimensional vector, and can naturally handle heterogeneous feature scales and missing values. For example, in the crypto LOB study, each \(T \times F\) sequence—where \(T\) is the number of time steps and \(F\) is the number of features—is flattened into a vector of length \(T \cdot F\), which is then fed into an XGBClassifier with a multi:softprob objective, using sample weights to correct class imbalance and early stopping on a validation set. With such setups and properly engineered inputs, gradient-boosted trees can capture complex relationships between order-book depth, recent returns, volatility, and OFI-like measures without recourse to explicit deep architectures.

Head-to-head comparisons between gradient-boosted trees and deep networks are relatively rare in equity LOB literature, but the crypto LOB BTC/USDT study provides a direct example. There, CatBoost and XGBoost models trained on engineered order-flow features and filtered LOB snapshots achieved accuracies on short-horizon movement prediction that matched or exceeded those of DeepLOB and Conv1D+LSTM architectures, suggesting that in highly noisy environments, the marginal gain from additional depth is modest once features are carefully constructed. The authors explicitly conclude that better inputs matter more than stacking another hidden layer, emphasizing the importance of data preprocessing, feature selection, and label design. This result aligns with observations from the inference–compute frontier study, where tree-based models occupy similar positions on the loss–compute frontier as deep architectures, reinforcing that gradient-boosted trees can be competitive in LOB prediction tasks when properly tuned.

In the FI‑2010 context, the inference–compute frontier paper shows that decision trees, histogram gradient boosting, CatBoost, and random-convolution logistic models collectively form a frontier that extrapolates well to excluded deep model targets, implying that tree-based models can approximate deep architectures’ performance at lower or comparable structural work. Although this study focuses on predictive loss rather than classification accuracy, its findings support the claim that gradient-boosted trees over raw LOB features can match deep models’ performance on FI‑2010 at some horizons, especially when the models are chosen optimally along the frontier. Together, these equity and crypto results provide empirical evidence that gradient-boosted trees can match deep models in short-horizon LOB forecasting, at least under certain feature and labeling configurations, and highlight the need to weigh complexity against compute and latency considerations in model selection.

### Order-flow features: OFI, microprice, and volume clocks

Order-flow imbalance (OFI), microprice, and volume clocks have long been recognized as influential microstructural features for short-horizon price prediction. OFI measures the net flow of aggressive buy and sell orders over a given interval, often computed across multiple depth levels to capture how incoming market orders and limit order cancellations shift the balance of supply and demand. Microprice integrates the idea that the “true” price is closer to the side with deeper liquidity, weighting the best bid and ask by their respective volumes to produce a price estimate that anticipates mean reversion toward the side with a thinner queue. Volume clocks, which scale time according to traded volume or queue volume dynamics, adjust for activity bursts and lulls, ensuring that models see comparable “information time” rather than uniform clock time.

While none of the deep architectures surveyed here use OFI or microprice as explicit inputs, they are implicitly sensitive to similar constructs through their handling of raw price and volume levels across depth and time. Gradient-boosted tree models, by contrast, often benefit from explicit OFI and microprice features, as they cannot automatically construct temporal convolutions or recurrent states; the crypto LOB study, for instance, augments baseline inputs with engineered order-flow imbalance across multiple depth levels, rolling volatility, and principal component summaries of the LOB snapshot, enabling tree-based models to approximate the feature abstractions that deep networks might learn. Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting and HLOB also discuss microstructural properties like tick size and liquidity in relation to predictability rates, implicitly acknowledging that better feature design could further enhance shallow models. Thus, while deep models can ingest raw LOB sequences and learn complex non-linear dependencies, gradient-boosted trees reach competitive performance only when fed rich, carefully engineered order-flow features, reinforcing the notion that feature quality and label design are as crucial as architectural depth in short-horizon LOB forecasting.

## Transfer Beyond NASDAQ Equities and Label Designs Under Transaction Costs

### Evidence of transfer beyond NASDAQ stocks

The question of transferability beyond NASDAQ equities—especially to index or FX futures with single tick sizes and deep books—is central to assessing whether deep LOB models and gradient-boosted trees can generalize across markets. Evidence from published works is mixed and, in many cases, limited. DeepLOB evaluates its CNN–LSTM architecture on both FI‑2010 (Nasdaq Nordic equities) and a proprietary LSE dataset, and reports stable out-of-sample prediction accuracy across a variety of instruments traded on the LSE, which suggests some in-equity-market transferability. However, these instruments are still equities, and the LSE dataset remains proprietary, so detailed insights into cross-market generalization are scarce. Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting and HLOB use NASDAQ equities from three datasets, including stocks with different tick sizes and liquidity profiles, and systematically characterize scenarios where deep models perform better or worse, but they do not extend their analysis to futures or FX markets.

TLOB and MLPLOB introduce cross-asset evaluations by testing on FI‑2010, NASDAQ stocks (Tesla and Intel), and a Bitcoin dataset, demonstrating that their architectures maintain or improve performance across these heterogeneous assets. BTC/USDT markets differ significantly from equities in trading patterns, volatility profiles, and tick structures, yet TLOB’s dual-attention mechanism and MLPLOB’s simple MLP architecture successfully adapt, outperforming prior models across horizons and markets. The crypto LOB study on BTC/USDT extends this cross-asset assessment by comparing deep and tree-based models under different labeling and filtering regimes, further illustrating that model rankings can change when microstructural contexts shift. Nevertheless, both these works focus on cryptocurrencies, which, while informative, are not index or FX futures and possess their own idiosyncratic fee structures and tick regimes.

LOBERT, as a foundation model, has the potential to transfer across markets due to its generic message-level representation; however, the published experiments emphasize equity markets and do not explicitly address futures or FX data. The inference–compute frontier study, too, is grounded in FI‑2010 and does not extend to futures or FX, though its methodological framework could theoretically be applied to other markets. Overall, the empirical record as of 2026 is that deep LOB models and gradient-boosted trees have been tested mostly on equity markets and, to a lesser extent, on cryptocurrencies; robust, publicly documented evidence of transfer to index or FX futures with single tick sizes and deep books is largely absent.

This absence likely reflects both data availability and microstructural differences. Futures and FX markets often have centralized limit order books with single tick sizes and deep depth, but access to historical message-level and multi-level LOB data can be proprietary, complicating public research. Moreover, the informational content of high-frequency LOB snapshots may differ in futures and FX due to the prevalence of algorithmic trading, large institutional flows, and cross-asset arbitrage strategies, which may reduce short-horizon predictability compared with equities or crypto assets. Pending large-scale studies that systematically apply deep LOB models and tree-based baselines to futures and FX LOB data, the best available evidence suggests that architectures like DeepLOB, HLOB, TLOB, LiT, and LOBERT can generalize across equity markets and to some extent to cryptocurrencies, but their performance and robustness on futures and FX remain open questions rather than established facts.

### Label designs and transaction costs of two ticks per side

Label design is pivotal for whether predicted signals can survive transaction costs, particularly when costs are on the order of two ticks per side, implying a four-tick round-trip spread that trades must overcome to be profitable. Most deep LOB works adopt k-step mid-price change labels with thresholds calibrated to small absolute changes or tick multiples, defining up/neutral/down classes based on whether the future mid-price at horizon \(h\) exceeds the current mid-price by a threshold \(\Delta\) or falls below it, with neutral covering near-zero changes. For FI‑2010 and similar datasets, thresholds are often set at one tick or very small price changes, which means that a significant portion of predicted “up” or “down” moves correspond to mid-price changes smaller than four ticks, making them unprofitable after paying two ticks per side. Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting explicitly confronts this issue by introducing an operational evaluation framework that focuses on forecasting complete transactions, assessing whether predicted directions lead to actual trades that cover transaction costs.

In their experiments, Briola, Bartolucci, and Aste show that the high forecasting power of deep learning models in terms of classification metrics does not necessarily correspond to actionable trading signals once transaction costs and execution uncertainty are included. Many correctly classified upward moves are too small to justify trading after costs, and the probability of correctly forecasting complete transactions—the “predictability rate”—is lower than raw accuracy suggests. This implies that label designs based on small thresholds (e.g., one tick) may not survive realistic transaction costs of two ticks per side, and that labels should be redefined such that “up” and “down” correspond to larger moves, perhaps exceeding four ticks, to align with economic viability. However, increasing thresholds reduces class balance and may diminish the apparent predictive power of models, creating a tension between statistical and economic significance.

TLOB directly grapples with this tension by introducing a new labeling method that removes horizon bias and incorporates transaction-cost considerations through trend thresholds based on average spread. By defining trends in relation to average spread, which serves as a proxy for transaction costs, the labels ensure that predicted “up” or “down” moves correspond to price changes that might realistically compensate for costs. Empirical results show that performance deteriorates when trend thresholds are defined using average spread, reflecting the complexity of translating trend classification into profitable trading strategies. This deterioration underscores that label designs aligned with two-tick-per-side costs are more demanding and that deep models’ apparent superiority under naive labels may shrink or vanish when thresholds are raised to economically meaningful levels.

HLOB’s analysis of information persistence across horizons offers additional insight into label design under transaction costs. For large-tick stocks, spatial information encoded in the LOB persists longer, and HLOB captures short-horizon movements (10 updates) effectively, outperforming other architectures in most scenarios. However, as horizons lengthen and informational content degrades, the probability of observing price moves large enough to clear a four-tick spread declines, further complicating the design of economically viable labels. Crypto LOB studies show similar patterns: in BTC/USDT, many short-horizon moves are small relative to fee structures and spreads, and classification performance must be interpreted in light of the net number of trades that beat costs. While these studies do not explicitly simulate two-tick-per-side cost scenarios, the underlying logic implies that labels must focus on sufficiently large moves—relative to spreads and fees—to be meaningful for trading.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that k-step mid-price change labels with small thresholds rarely survive transaction costs of about two ticks per side in a straightforward way. Label designs that explicitly incorporate spread-based thresholds, like TLOB’s average-spread-centric labels, better align with transactional viability but lead to lower classification performance and highlight the fragility of earlier high-accuracy results. A promising direction, as outlined in Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting, is the use of operational labels tied to complete transactions, where the label encodes not just direction but the feasibility of executing a profitable round-trip trade after costs, though this area remains under-explored in published deep LOB literature.

## Replication, Robustness, and Unreproduced Claims

### FI‑2010 and the fragility of early DeepLOB results

One of the recurring themes in recent LOB research is that early deep learning results on FI‑2010 are fragile and difficult to replicate under varied conditions. DeepLOB initially reported state-of-the-art performance on FI‑2010, substantially outperforming logistic regression and other machine learning baselines, but later studies revealed that these results were sensitive to specific choices of feature conventions, horizon labels, class balancing, and train–test splits. The inference–compute frontier paper explicitly notes that FI‑2010’s feature conventions, horizon labels, class balance, and reproduced rankings require careful reporting, implying that discrepancies in these choices have led to inconsistent reproductions of earlier rankings. Briola, Turiel, and Aste’s comparative perspective on deep learning modeling of limit order books, referenced in the Emergent Mind review, similarly suggests that different deep architectures can fare differently depending on dataset and labeling choices, complicating claims of universal superiority.

Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting revisits deep models on new NASDAQ datasets and underscores that microstructural characteristics like tick size and liquidity strongly influence performance, such that high accuracy on FI‑2010 does not automatically translate to other equity markets. While this does not constitute a “failed replication” of DeepLOB’s FI‑2010 results, it does demonstrate that the high-performance claims are context-dependent and should not be generalized without microstructural analysis. LOBFrame’s emphasis on reproducibility and accessibility—integrating state-of-the-art insights into a cohesive system and releasing code—reflects an acknowledgement that earlier proprietary datasets and non-reproducible methodologies hindered independent verification of DeepLOB and related models. GitHub reimplementations of DeepLOB, such as Yuxi Huan’s project, have replicated the architecture on FI‑2010, but provide limited publicly documented comparisons to original baselines, making it difficult to fully assess replication fidelity.

Overall, the consensus emerging from LOBFrame, HLOB, and the inference–compute frontier is that DeepLOB’s FI‑2010 performance should be seen as an early proof of concept rather than a definitive benchmark, and that subsequent studies have revised downward the implied generality of its success. In particular, when transaction-cost-aware evaluation and microstructural heterogeneity are considered, deep models’ dominance shrinks, and tree-based models can often match or approximate performance, suggesting that early claims of transformative predictive gains were partly driven by unrealistic labeling and evaluation protocols.

### Declining predictability and market efficiency

TLOB’s finding that stock price predictability has declined over time, with an empirical drop of approximately 6.68 F1-score points, adds a temporal dimension to replication challenges. This decline, interpreted as evidence of growing market efficiency, implies that models trained on older datasets like FI‑2010 may overstate the achievable performance on contemporary markets, and that replications on recent data will naturally exhibit lower predictability even if methodological choices are sound. In this sense, the failure to replicate earlier high F1 scores is not necessarily due to methodological flaws but to structural changes in the markets themselves, including increased algorithmic trading, tighter spreads, and more sophisticated arbitrage, which reduce exploitable microstructural patterns.

The crypto LOB study also points to temporal and structural changes in cryptocurrency markets, where early deep learning models might have exploited inefficiencies that have since diminished, though this is less explicitly documented than in equities. Combined with HLOB’s analysis of informational degradation over longer horizons and across tick-size categories, these findings collectively suggest that any replication of early deep model performance must consider the evolving nature of market microstructure and that static benchmarks like FI‑2010 can mislead if treated as representative of current conditions.

### Gradient-boosted trees and the re-evaluation of depth

The crypto LOB study’s conclusion that better inputs matter more than stacking another hidden layer can be seen as a kind of “failed replication” of deep models’ claimed superiority. When logistic regression, XGBoost, and CatBoost are equipped with rich order-flow features and data filtering, they match or surpass deep architectures like DeepLOB and Conv1D+LSTM on BTC/USDT, challenging the notion that depth is intrinsically advantageous in noisy microstructural environments. Similarly, the inference–compute frontier shows that tree-based models occupy positions along the loss–compute frontier comparable to deep architectures, suggesting that much of the perceived advantage of deep models arises from naive feature baselines rather than fundamental limitations of tree-based methods.

These results prompt a re-evaluation of deep architectures in LOB forecasting and underscore that published deep model results can fail to replicate when head-to-head comparisons against well-constructed gradient-boosted baselines are performed. While this failure is not absolute—deep models retain advantages in modeling complex spatial–temporal dependencies and in flexible transfer learning— it is sufficient to question early claims that deep architectures are strictly necessary for short-horizon LOB prediction and to advocate for a more balanced view that weighs feature quality, label design, compute, and latency alongside model depth.

### Blogs, informal replications, and simulation studies

Beyond formal papers, blogs and informal reports have attempted to replicate LOB deep learning results, often using publicly available code and data. The Emergent Mind review “Limit Order Book Simulations: A Review” references Briola, Turiel, and Aste’s comparative perspective on deep learning modeling of LOBs and situates these works within broader simulation and modeling efforts. While this review is itself a blog-style synthesis rather than a primary research article, it highlights that simulation-based studies often find that deep models can overfit to specific microstructural regimes and that simple models can perform adequately when simulation parameters are tuned to realistic levels. Such informal replications and simulations, while not definitive, contribute to the broader narrative that early deep LOB results must be interpreted cautiously and that robust benchmarks and transparent code, like those in LOBFrame and TLOB repositories, are essential for reliable evaluation.

In summary, the replication record as of 2026 is mixed. Some early deep LOB results, particularly on FI‑2010, have proven fragile and context-dependent, with subsequent studies highlighting the importance of microstructural heterogeneity, label design, and transaction-cost-aware evaluation. Gradient-boosted trees and simpler models have, in some cases, matched or exceeded deep architectures when fed better features and filtered data, prompting a reconsideration of depth as a necessary condition for short-horizon LOB forecasting. Temporal changes in market efficiency further complicate replication, as older datasets may simply be less representative of current microstructural patterns. Together, these factors underscore the need for cautious interpretation of published results and for future research that emphasizes reproducibility, cross-market transfer, and cost-aware evaluation.

## Conclusion

As of 2026, the landscape of deep learning and gradient-boosting methods for one-to-five-minute price forecasting from limit order book and trade data is both rich and sobering. DeepLOB inaugurated the era of large-scale CNN–LSTM models on LOB snapshots, demonstrating that deep architectures could substantially outperform logistic regression and other traditional machine learning methods on benchmark datasets like FI‑2010. Subsequent developments, including LOBFrame and HLOB, refined our understanding of microstructural influences on predictability and introduced sophisticated architectures that exploit higher-order spatial dependencies and information-filtering networks, while simultaneously emphasizing that classification metrics alone do not guarantee profitable trading once transaction costs are considered.

Transformer-based architectures such as TLOB and LiT extended this progress by modeling spatial and temporal relationships in LOB data through dual-attention and patch-based mechanisms, achieving state-of-the-art performance across equity and cryptocurrency datasets and introducing label designs that incorporate spread-based thresholds to better align predictions with transaction-cost realities. Foundation models like LOBERT further broadened the scope by modeling LOB dynamics at the message level, learning general representations of market microstructure that can be fine-tuned for mid-price movement and next-message prediction tasks. The inference–compute frontier study introduced FastBiNLOB and showed that structural forward work provides a useful capacity coordinate for heterogeneous LOB predictors, revealing a power-law frontier that tree-based models and deep architectures jointly occupy.

At the same time, comparative studies in cryptocurrency markets and head-to-head evaluations in equity LOB contexts have revealed that gradient-boosted trees over carefully engineered order-flow features can match or even exceed deep models at short horizons, especially when data preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning are carefully performed. These results challenge the necessity of deep architectures in some settings and highlight that better inputs often matter more than added depth, particularly under tight latency and interpretability constraints. Evidence of transfer beyond NASDAQ equities to index or FX futures remains limited; while deep models and tree-based methods have demonstrated robustness across equity markets and into cryptocurrencies, public, large-scale studies on futures and FX LOB data are sparse, leaving generalization to these markets an open empirical question.

On label design, the consensus is that naive k-step mid-price change labels with small thresholds rarely survive transaction costs of two ticks per side, and that labels must be redefined in relation to spreads and execution costs to yield economically meaningful predictions. When this is done—through spread-based thresholds or transaction-aware labels—classification performance typically deteriorates, revealing that much of the apparent predictive power in early deep model results was tied to economically trivial moves. Replication efforts have further shown that early high-performance claims on FI‑2010 are fragile, dependent on specific feature and label conventions, and do not necessarily generalize to more recent datasets or different markets, particularly as market efficiency has increased over time.

Looking ahead, the most promising directions for LOB forecasting research involve integrating deep and gradient-boosted approaches within standardized frameworks like LOBFrame; expanding datasets to include index and FX futures with deep books and single tick sizes; developing cost-aware and transaction-centric label designs; and exploiting foundation models like LOBERT for multitask and cross-market transfer. Ultimately, the value of any model in this domain will be judged not only by its performance on benchmark classification tasks but by its ability to produce robust, net-profitable trading signals under realistic microstructural and transaction-cost conditions. The work surveyed here provides essential building blocks for that endeavor, while also offering a cautionary tale about the risks of overinterpreting high F1 scores on narrow benchmarks without careful attention to microstructure, costs, and replication.
