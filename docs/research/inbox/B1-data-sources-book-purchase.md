# Historical Data Acquisition for B3 Futures and Equities: Daily, Intraday and Order-Book Sources as of 2026

The Brazilian exchange B3 offers a diverse ecosystem of official and third‑party data products, ranging from free end‑of‑day quotations to highly granular order‑book and participant‑level information distributed through licensed vendors and proprietary platforms, and building a historical‑only research pipeline for mini index futures (WIN), mini dollar futures (WDO) and leading IBOV equities requires a careful mapping of this ecosystem as it stands by the mid‑2020s. The most robust free foundation for daily bars in equities is the long‑running COTAHIST file family, whose layout and history are well documented and which can be accessed either directly from B3 or via open‑source tooling such as the R package rb3. Intraday one‑minute bars and tick‑by‑tick trades are more fragmented: B3’s UP2DATA and UP2DATA ON DEMAND services formally cover end‑of‑day reference prices, open positions and intraday data, while retail trading front‑ends such as MetaTrader 5, Profit (Nelogica) and Tryd provide access to live and limited historical intraday series tied to specific brokers, and licensed distributors and global vendors add commercial offerings that are not uniformly documented in public sources. The greatest opacity surrounds historical order‑book (“ofertas”) and participant‑coded trade data, where legacy public files such as NEGOCIOSAVISTA and OFER_CPA/OFER_VDA are referenced in glossaries but their historical availability, discontinuation dates and current replacements must be inferred indirectly from the description of B3’s modern data services and distributor network. Throughout this report, each identified source is analysed in terms of granularity, instrument coverage, depth of history, file format and schema, approximate data volume for WIN and WDO, pricing and licensing constraints, and feasibility for personal academic research under realistic budget constraints, with explicit indication of every fact that could not be verified from public documentation.

## The B3 Data Landscape and Research Requirements

### Institutional context and instrument focus

Any discussion of historical market data from B3 must begin with a clear understanding of the trading segments and instruments involved and of the exchange’s approach to public versus commercial data dissemination. B3 is the integrated Brazilian exchange formed from the merger of BM&FBovespa and Cetip, and it now operates cash equities, equity derivatives, currency and interest‑rate derivatives, commodities and credit instruments on unified trading and post‑trading platforms. The mini index future WIN is a futures contract on the Ibovespa index traded in the derivatives segment, while the mini dollar WDO is a futures contract on the U.S. dollar versus Brazilian real; both are among the most liquid contracts on the exchange and are widely used by retail and professional traders, including via automated trading solutions. The approximately twenty most liquid Ibovespa constituents are large‑capitalisation Brazilian equities traded on the cash market, and their historical data is overwhelmingly recorded in the COTAHIST daily quotation files and in intraday trade and order‑book feeds for the equities segment.

From a data architecture perspective, B3 explicitly distinguishes between three major classes of information in UP2DATA: security list data describing instruments and their registration and eligibility as collateral, open positions reflecting the total inventory of securities by individual security across participants, and trade information summarising daily trading activity for each security authorised for electronic trading. The trade information channel includes variables such as last trade price, minimum and maximum prices, traded volume and number of traded contracts, and is made available in standardised formats such as TXT, CSV, XML and JSON designed for automated consumption by investor‑side software and cloud environments. For more advanced analytics and strategic corporate decision‑making, B3’s Datawise and Datawise Insights products consolidate sensitive and market data into interactive dashboards and reports, including highly thematic products like the “Retirees X‑Ray” panel that combines public IBGE and Social Security series with B3’s own data for demographic and benefit analysis. These products illustrate the broader trend toward analytics‑oriented data services whose raw feeds may not be directly exposed to retail researchers in the same way as legacy text files such as COTAHIST or NEGOCIOSAVISTA.

The research pipeline envisaged for WIN, WDO and leading IBOV stocks emphasizes historical analysis rather than live trading, and it must operate within the constraints of a single desktop machine with roughly one terabyte of disk space. This capacity is ample for daily bars and moderate volumes of intraday trade data, but order‑book data at offers‑level granularity can be extremely voluminous, particularly for very active futures contracts, and may require careful selection of instruments and time windows. Because the focus is on historical data only, timeliness requirements such as the T‑1 or T‑2 updating cycles described for Datawise+ are less critical, yet understanding these cycles helps clarify when B3 considers data “final” for historical purposes. The pipeline must ultimately balance free official sources, free and semi‑free data embedded in trading platforms, and commercial data purchased from B3 or third‑party vendors, while taking into account licensing conditions that may restrict redistribution or use beyond strictly personal research.

A complicating factor is that some of B3’s more granular public data products have evolved over time, with older file families such as daily NEGOCIOSAVISTA or OFER_CPA/OFER_VDA being referenced in glossaries and documentation but with limited information on when they ceased to be freely downloadable or how they were folded into the current architecture of UP2DATA and UP2DATA ON DEMAND. This opacity makes it necessary to infer the contemporary state of play from what is stated explicitly in FAQs, helpdesk documents and marketing material for Datawise and UP2DATA, and from the list of licensed distributors who act as intermediaries for data products whose structure and pricing are negotiated bilaterally. In this context, drawing on the rb3 open‑source package is particularly helpful, because its maintainers have reverse‑engineered the structure and availability of COTAHIST and other public files by web‑scraping the B3 website and making the results available in R, and their documentation offers practical guidance for academic users without commercial budgets.

The sections that follow organise the data acquisition landscape into daily quotation files, intraday trade and bar data, order‑book and offers‑level data, and broker or participant identifiers on trades. Within each category, both official exchange products and platform‑embedded or third‑party offerings are described with an emphasis on the attributes requested: granularity, instrument coverage, history depth, file formats and schema, approximate size per month for WIN and WDO, and any information on pricing and licensing that can be gleaned from public sources. Wherever facts could not be reliably verified—for example, the precise historical depth of intraday data on retail platforms or the exact pricing tiers for UP2DATA ON DEMAND—they are flagged explicitly to enable a realistic expectation of what can and cannot be known without contacting vendors directly.

## Daily Bar Data: COTAHIST and Other Free Sources

### Structure and history of COTAHIST files

The cornerstone of free daily bar data for B3‑listed instruments is the COTAHIST family of text files, which B3 describes as containing historical quotation data relative to the negotiation of all financial market papers during a one‑year period and which are organised by year under file names such as COTAHIST.1990.TXT, COTAHIST.1991.TXT and so on. Each COTAHIST file is a fixed‑width text file with a register size of 245 bytes per line and consists of three types of records: header records identified by type code 00, daily paper quotation records with type code 01, and a trailer record with type code 99. The daily quotation records, which are the ones relevant for historical bars, contain a rich set of fields including the type of register, trading date, BDI (Daily Information Bulletin) code, company name, negotiation code (ticker), market type, short company name, and a series of price and volume fields such as opening price, maximum price, minimum price and last trade price.

The unofficial layout documentation maintained in the curiosity repository on GitHub confirms and extends the description from B3’s own file layout PDF, noting that each COTAHIST line has exactly 245 characters and that the file follows a positional layout, with each field occupying fixed start and end positions along the line. For example, the field TIPREG occupies positions 1 to 2 and is always "01" for detail records, the trading date DATPRE occupies positions 3 to 10 and is formatted as AAAAMMDD, the BDI code CODBDI occupies positions 11 to 12, the ticker CODNEG occupies positions 13 to 24, and the market type TPMERC occupies positions 25 to 27. The short company name NOMRES occupies positions 28 to 39, and price fields such as opening price PREABE, maximum price PREMAX, minimum price PREMIN and last trade price PREULT occupy ranges 57–69, 70–82, 83–95 and 109–121 respectively, with the prices recorded as integers with two implicit decimal places. In practical terms, a PREULT field containing "0000000003850" corresponds to a closing price of 38.50 Brazilian reais, and the implied conversion formula for all such price fields is simply that the real price equals the integer field value divided by 100.

The rb3 package documentation corroborates that COTAHIST files contain historical quotation data for a wide range of instruments including stocks, stock options, stock forward contracts, exchange‑traded funds (ETFs), ETF options, Brazilian Depositary Receipts (BDRs), UNITs, real estate investment funds (FIIs), agricultural industry funds (FIAGROs) and FIDCs, and it emphasises that these files hold the oldest available information for these instruments on B3. The earliest annual COTAHIST file rb3 has located dates back to 1986, though rb3 cautions that data prior to 1995 should be used with care due to Brazil’s monetary stabilisation in 1994 under the Plano Real, which affects the interpretation of nominal price series. Importantly, rb3 notes that prices in COTAHIST files are not adjusted for corporate actions, and as a result only ETF series can generally be used without adjustment issues; for individual equities and other instruments, users must apply their own splits and dividend adjustments to construct continuous series.

In terms of accessibility and automation, rb3 shows that COTAHIST files can be programmatically downloaded and parsed using its functions download_marketdata, read_marketdata, fetch_marketdata and cotahist_get. A typical workflow involves using fetch_marketdata with a template such as "b3‑cotahist‑yearly" and specifying a vector of years, for example from 2000 to 2025, to download multiple annual files. These files are then read into R using read_marketdata and converted into structured datasets with cotahist_get, which accepts arguments specifying the period type ("yearly", "monthly", "daily"). The documentation illustrates code patterns such as `fetch_marketdata("b3-cotahist-yearly", year = 2023)` followed by `ch <- cotahist_get("yearly")` and subsequent filtering for equities via helper functions such as cotahist_filter_equity, enabling academic users to extract daily bar series for specific instruments over long periods without manual handling of raw text files.

For the purpose of a research pipeline focused on the twenty most liquid Ibovespa stocks, COTAHIST provides complete daily open, high, low, last and volume bars for the entire period from roughly the mid‑1980s to the present, subject to the caveats about pre‑1995 monetary instability and unadjusted prices. Because COTAHIST includes the market type TPMERC and BDI classification, the pipeline can also filter for regular cash market trades (for example, TPMERC 010 for the main board versus TPMERC 020 for fractional trading) and for standard lot instruments (BDI code 02) versus other categories such as real estate funds (BDI code 12). This granularity in metadata is important when constructing clean equity universes that exclude non‑regular instruments and when reconciling daily bars with intraday data that may be segmented by market type.

### Other free daily bar sources and limitations

Beyond COTAHIST, B3’s contemporary data services emphasise end‑of‑day summaries rather than generic daily bar files, but these can serve as alternative or supplementary sources for specific purposes. The UP2DATA product is described by B3 as providing end‑of‑day market data and reference data in an organised, consolidated and standardised manner, with data made available by channels corresponding to different information types. Within UP2DATA, the "Trade Information" channel delivers summarised information about the activities of each security authorised for trading on B3’s electronic systems, including last trade price, minimum price, maximum price, traded volume and number of traded contracts, among other variables. While UP2DATA is primarily marketed as a commercial service, its samples and documentation indicate that the daily trade information for equities and derivatives can, in principle, be mapped into daily bar structures analogous to those in COTAHIST, albeit with potentially different field names and formats.

The UP2DATA FAQs and helpdesk documentation explain that clients can download UP2DATA files in TXT, CSV, XML and JSON formats and that the service is accessed either via an installed software client on a company server or through configuration of a cloud environment to receive files, with access authenticated by installation keys and SAS keys and validated by certificates. These certificates prove that a given client has authorisation to access UP2DATA services and channels, are unique per client, and are valid for two years, while product keys are also required and have validity periods that differ between client and cloud versions. Because individual retail researchers typically do not operate B3‑certified servers or cloud configurations, UP2DATA is more suited to institutional users, and there is no public indication that its daily trade information summaries for equities are available free of charge or that they can be easily accessed by private individuals without entering into a commercial agreement. As a result, for the kind of historical daily bar work envisaged here, COTAHIST remains the primary free source.

Trading platforms such as Profit Pro by Nelogica and other front‑ends like Tryd offer daily quotation grids and summaries for instruments, but these are generally designed for interactive visualisation rather than bulk historical download. The Nelogica blog describes the “Grade de Cotações” feature in Profit Pro as enabling the visualisation of key attributes of assets, such as maximum and minimum prices, volume and previous closing, effectively providing a daily snapshot grid of these variables. Similarly, the MacOS documentation for Profit mentions comprehensive "Livro de Ofertas" and "Monitor de Mercado" features that show offer books, price books, and other live attributes, but it does not explicitly describe historical export capabilities for daily bars. Because this documentation is marketing‑oriented and does not specify file formats or available history depths, no verified claim can be made, based on public sources, that Profit or Tryd provide bulk historical daily bar exports suitable for long‑term research.

Third‑party financial data vendors, including those listed by B3 as licensed distributors for equities and options and for futures and foreign exchange, almost certainly provide daily bar data as part of their broader data offerings, but again the public list of distributors focuses on contact information and instrument categories rather than on detailed descriptions of data products. The distributor list includes entities such as Bloomberg Finance LP, CMA Consultoria, Avelacom, Beyond Soluções, Tryd’s parent Guanahaní Participações, and many others, with columns indicating whether they cover equities and options or futures and foreign exchange, thereby signalling their role as intermediaries in disseminating B3 market data. However, the list does not specify whether daily bar data is sold separately from intraday data, nor does it provide pricing, history depth or licensing terms for personal research use. Consequently, while daily bar series are almost certainly available from these vendors in convenient formats and with corporate action adjustments, such offerings cannot be enumerated in detail from the information presently accessible.

### Data volume and storage considerations for daily bars

From a storage perspective, daily bar data for roughly twenty equities over a multi‑decade period is modest relative to contemporary disk capacities and presents no constraint for a one‑terabyte desktop. A single COTAHIST daily detail line for an equity occupies 245 bytes, and a trading day on B3 involves at most one such line per instrument per day in the COTAHIST files, because each line represents the summary of all trading for that day. For twenty equities over approximately thirty‑five years, with roughly 250 trading days per year, the total number of lines would be \(20 \times 35 \times 250 = 175{,}000\). Multiplying by 245 bytes yields roughly \(175{,}000 \times 245 \approx 42.9\) megabytes of raw COTAHIST text data for the equities of interest, which is negligible compared to one terabyte and remains small even when parsed into higher‑level data structures. Even if the pipeline includes all equities in COTAHIST for that period for cross‑sectional studies, the total data size remains well below a few gigabytes, given that COTAHIST compresses daily quotes across many instruments in a compact fixed‑width layout.

For futures contracts such as WIN and WDO, daily bars from COTAHIST are similarly lightweight, although users must be careful about contract rolling, because COTAHIST records data at the contract level rather than on a continuous series basis. Each front‑month futures contract appears with its own ticker, and a research pipeline must either handle contracts individually or pre‑construct continuous series by rolling based on expiry dates and liquidity patterns. This contract‑level structure does not materially affect storage requirements; it simply increases the number of COTAHIST lines involved and the complexity of the mapping between tickers and the intended economic series. Given one terabyte of disk, storing daily bars for all WIN and WDO contracts over many years is trivial and does not require specialised compression or partitioning schemes.

Overall, the COTAHIST files, together with potential daily summaries from UP2DATA if commercial access is obtained, provide a robust and compact foundation for daily bar research on B3. The principal challenges lie in the lack of corporate action adjustment in COTAHIST, the need to filter for regular market segments and instrument types, and the limited information on alternative free daily bar sources from trading platforms. For an academic researcher seeking to minimise costs, building daily bars from COTAHIST via rb3 is the dominant strategy and enjoys strong public documentation and community support.

## Intraday One‑Minute Bars and Tick‑by‑Tick Trades

### B3’s official intraday and end‑of‑day products

Intraday data, including one‑minute bars and individual trades, are more complex than daily bars because they are generally not released in bulk in free public files, and access often requires subscription to data services or use of broker‑provided feeds. B3’s UP2DATA product is explicitly designed to provide end‑of‑day market data and reference data, as well as economic indicators, in an organised, consolidated and standardised manner, and it spans data on securities registration, open interest, trade information, settlement and reference prices, and economic indicators for commodities, interest rates, currencies and equities. While the primary emphasis is on end‑of‑day aggregates, which might correspond to daily bars, the UP2DATA ON DEMAND extension is described as offering retroactive data on closing prices, reference prices, settlement prices, instrument registration, indices, intraday data and open positions of B3’s securities in the commodities, interest rate, currency and equity segments.

The UP2DATA ON DEMAND service is marketed as an online data store created to provide quality and reliable information to users and to support more accurate market analyses, such as predictive analysis, risk analysis, investment strategies, economic models and academic studies. B3 states that UP2DATA ON DEMAND consolidates data packages with twenty years of history of the markets managed by B3 and that these packages can be customised and downloaded by anyone—investors, financial market professionals or academic researchers—through a simple online purchase. It explicitly notes that UP2DATA ON DEMAND offers retroactive data on closing prices, reference prices, settlement prices, instrument registration, indices, intraday data and open positions for a wide variety of securities. However, neither the UP2DATA ON DEMAND marketing page nor the client information note on the entry of CRI and CRA data in the service provides explicit details about the exact granularity of intraday data (for example, whether it includes one‑minute bars or tick‑by‑tick trades) or about the file formats used for intraday series.

In terms of technical infrastructure, the broader UP2DATA product supports file downloads in TXT, CSV, XML and JSON formats, and access is mediated either via an installed UP2DATA Client on a company server or via a cloud environment with an UP2DATA Cloud reader, both of which rely on access keys and certificates for authentication. Certificates are valid for two years and prove that a client has authorisation to access UP2DATA services and channels, while installation keys for the server client are valid for two years and cloud keys for the UP2DATA Cloud are valid for ninety days, with cloud keys renewable via API. Although these details apply primarily to subscription‑based UP2DATA rather than to UP2DATA ON DEMAND, they illustrate B3’s preference for controlled access even for intraday data offered through on‑demand packages. The available public documentation does not state whether intraday data purchased through UP2DATA ON DEMAND is delivered via the same channels and formats as subscription UP2DATA or whether it is provided as downloadable archives in standard formats accessible to individuals. Consequently, while it is reasonable to infer that intraday data for futures contracts such as WIN and WDO and for equities can be obtained from UP2DATA ON DEMAND in some machine‑readable format, the precise granularity and schema are not documented in the sources at hand and cannot be verified without contacting B3 or accessing the service directly.

Datawise, B3’s analytics‑oriented product suite, focuses more on interactive dashboards and reports than on raw intraday series, at least in the publicly documented offerings. The "Retirees X‑Ray" product, for example, provides panels for the historical series of absolute values in billions of reais, percentages of total benefits and percentages relative to other variables for retirement benefits across Brazilian regions and municipalities, with data filterable from January 2007 to M‑3. These panels cover macroeconomic and demographic series, not market trades or order books, and they are intended for visual analysis rather than for bulk data extraction. Datawise more broadly is described as helping users chart corporate strategies based on real data from the markets served by B3, and offering comparative data dashboards with graphs and information relevant to decision‑making. No public documentation indicates that Datawise provides downloadable tick‑by‑tick trade data or even one‑minute intraday bar files for WIN and WDO, as opposed to aggregated and filtered views accessible through their dashboards.

Consequently, the most plausible official path to intraday data for futures and equities is via the UP2DATA ON DEMAND service, which explicitly advertises intraday data and open positions across B3 markets and is positioned as suitable for academic research. However, the sources available do not specify whether intraday data is offered at one‑minute bar granularity, tick‑by‑tick trade level, or some more aggregated resolution, nor do they provide pricing per instrument or per month, or licensing restrictions beyond the general framework governing B3 data services. Moreover, the documentation does not state whether buyer and seller participant codes, or order‑book events, are included in intraday datasets, leaving these attributes uncertain from publicly accessible information.

### Approximate data volumes for WIN and WDO intraday series

While official documentation does not provide explicit statistics on the number of trades or order‑book events per day for WIN and WDO, some general approximations can be made to assess storage requirements, understanding that they are not empirically verified for B3. Mini index and mini dollar futures are among the most actively traded contracts on B3, with typical trading days involving hundreds of thousands of trades and many millions of order‑book events, particularly during peak volatility. If one assumes, for illustrative purposes, that WIN and WDO each generate on the order of 200,000 trades per day and that each trade record stored in a CSV file occupies approximately 100 bytes, then a day’s worth of tick‑by‑tick trades for both instruments might consume around \(2 \times 200{,}000 \times 100 = 40\) megabytes. Over a 22‑day trading month, this would scale to roughly 880 megabytes, and over 12 months to around 10.5 gigabytes. These magnitudes are manageable within a one‑terabyte disk, even accounting for overhead from parsing and analysis, but they illustrate that intraday data series are considerably more voluminous than daily bars.

One‑minute bars, by contrast, compress intraday information substantially. A typical trading day for WIN or WDO might have around 390 one‑minute intervals if the instrument trades continuously from roughly 9:00 to 15:50 plus after‑hours sessions, so each instrument would generate fewer than 1,000 one‑minute bars per day. Assuming 22 trading days per month, this yields under 22,000 bars per instrument per month, or 44,000 bars for both contracts. At a record size of, say, 100 bytes per bar—covering timestamp, open, high, low, close and volume—the raw one‑minute bar data would occupy under 4.4 megabytes per month for both WIN and WDO. Over 12 months, this would amount to under 53 megabytes, which is trivial in storage terms. These calculations are illustrative and not grounded in B3‑specific documentation; they rely on general market behaviour and typical file sizes and must be treated as unverified approximations intended solely for planning purposes.

From a practical standpoint, the relatively small size of one‑minute bars makes them attractive for initial research, especially when order‑book data is either unavailable or prohibitively expensive. Tick‑by‑tick trade data is larger but still manageable for a small number of instruments over a limited time window. Order‑book data at offers‑level granularity, which includes add, modify and cancel events for each price level, can easily exceed tens of gigabytes per instrument per month, depending on activity, and thus requires more careful planning and potentially selective capture or compression. Without documented average event rates for WIN and WDO from B3, these considerations remain qualitative, but they reinforce the importance of focusing on a small instrument set and a limited horizon (six to twelve months) when working with order‑book data on a one‑terabyte desktop.

## Order‑Book and Offers‑Level Data

### Legacy public files: NEG and OFER_CPA/OFER_VDA

Historically, B3 and its predecessor BM&FBovespa made detailed trade and order‑book data available in daily text files such as NEGOCIOSAVISTA for cash equities and OFER_CPA/OFER_VDA for offers, which included, at least in some segments, buyer and seller participant codes and order‑book events. The contemporary glossary for public data service files confirms the existence of the file DD‑MM‑AAAA_NEGOCIOSAVISTA, described as reporting all trades carried out during the current trading session at the level of individual trades, thereby bringing transparency of trades to the market. The glossary notes that the NEGOCIOSAVISTA file is provided as a zipped folder containing a .txt file in a specified tabular format, and that fields in the file include buyer participant code (CodigoParticipanteComprador, BuyerParticipantCode) and seller participant code (CodigoParticipanteVendedor, SellerParticipantCode). However, it also states that for agro‑derivatives, all offers and operations registered in the negotiation system are disclosed in an anonymous manner, without identification of the trading participant’s code. This indicates that participant codes are available in some segments but anonymised in others.

### Data formats, granularity and size considerations for order‑book data

From the perspective of a research pipeline for WIN and WDO, order‑book data can be represented either as event‑level streams, documenting each add, modify, cancel and trade event at each price level, or as periodic snapshots capturing the state of the book at fixed intervals. Event‑level streams, particularly for active futures, can generate large volumes of data. If one assumes unverified rough magnitudes such as several million events per trading day per instrument, with each event occupying roughly 100 bytes, then a single instrument’s order‑book events could produce hundreds of megabytes per day. Over a month of 22 trading days, this could exceed several gigabytes per instrument, and over a year, tens of gigabytes for just WIN and WDO. On a one‑terabyte disk, storing six to twelve months of event‑level order‑book data for both instruments is feasible but would require careful management, compression and partitioning to avoid performance bottlenecks.

Snapshot‑based representations, for example capturing the top 10 levels of the order book every second or every five seconds, dramatically reduce data volume while still conveying rich information about liquidity and order‑flow dynamics. If one captures book snapshots at one‑second intervals during a trading day of roughly seven hours (25,200 seconds), and records the top 10 bid and ask levels for each snapshot, then a single instrument yields 25,200 records per day. Assuming 22 trading days per month, this translates to around 554,400 snapshots per month per instrument, or about 1.1 million snapshots for both WIN and WDO combined. At a record size of 200 bytes capturing price, quantity and other metadata for 20 levels, the monthly data volume would be roughly 220 megabytes for both instruments. Over 12 months, this scales to under three gigabytes, which is very manageable on a one‑terabyte disk. Again, these figures are illustrative and not derived from B3 documentation; they are included to aid planning but must be treated as approximations not tied to documented B3 order‑book event rates or file sizes.

The choice between event‑level and snapshot‑level data is influenced by both research goals and data availability. B3’s UP2DATA ON DEMAND explicitly advertises intraday data and open positions but does not specify whether this includes full event streams. Many third‑party vendors offer both formats, with pricing reflecting the higher storage and transmission costs of event‑level data. Given the budget constraint of roughly R$2,000 for a six‑ to twelve‑month order‑book purchase, snapshot‑level data may be more affordable, particularly when limited to two instruments, and may be more realistic to store and process on a desktop machine. ## Quantitative Planning: Storage, Formats and Schema Integration

### File formats and schema integration across sources

The research pipeline must integrate data from multiple sources, each with its own file format and schema conventions, into a coherent relational or time‑series database suitable for quantitative analysis. COTAHIST daily quotation files are fixed‑width text files with positional layouts, where each line has 245 characters and fields such as ticker, date, BDI code, market type and price variables occupy defined ranges. By contrast, UP2DATA files can be downloaded in TXT, CSV, XML or JSON formats, with channel‑specific structures; the Trade Information channel summarises last trade price, minimum and maximum prices, traded volume and number of contracts, while Security List and Open Positions channels carry instrument metadata and inventory data. Trading platforms such as MetaTrader 5, Profit and Tryd likely export data in CSV or proprietary binary formats, though this is not documented in the sources at hand. Third‑party vendors generally offer CSV, JSON, or specialised time‑series formats.

To harmonise these formats, the pipeline must define canonical schemas for daily bars, intraday bars, tick‑by‑tick trades and order‑book events. For daily bars, the schema can map COTAHIST fields to generic variables such as date, ticker, open, high, low, close, volume and perhaps number of trades, while preserving additional metadata such as BDI and market type. For intraday one‑minute bars and tick‑by‑tick trades, canonical fields include timestamp, instrument code, last price, quantity, side (BUYI or SELL), and possibly trade identifier, aggressor flag and venue. For order‑book events, canonical fields encompass timestamp, instrument, side (bid or ask), price level, quantity, event type (add, modify, cancel, trade) and possibly participant code. Participant identities, if available, can be mapped to separate tables with attributes such as broker name and classification, leveraging codes like 3‑123456 to represent trading participants.

Because COTAHIST uses implicit two‑decimal integer prices, all integration pipelines must convert these fields using the documented formula, dividing by 100 to obtain real prices. UP2DATA, by contrast, likely uses decimal representations in CSV and JSON, although this is not explicitly documented in the sources at hand. Trading platforms and third‑party vendors may use floating‑point representations. Care must be taken to avoid rounding errors and to maintain precise tick values for futures, where price increment sizes are fixed. Package tools such as rb3 in R, which parse COTAHIST into numeric fields, can be used to abstract away some of these conversion details.

Schema integration also involves mapping instrument codes across sources. For equities, COTAHIST uses CODNEG as the negotiation code (ticker), while UP2DATA may use instrument identifiers and ISINs. For futures, instruments such as WIN and WDO may have contract codes that include expiry month and year alongside root symbols, requiring careful mapping to continuous series. Security List data in UP2DATA can help identify instrument attributes and relationships. In addition, consistent time zones and trading session definitions must be used when aligning intraday and daily data, particularly given Brazil’s daylight saving policies and periodic changes in trading hours.

### Storage planning and indexing strategies

With one terabyte of disk space, the pipeline can comfortably accommodate daily and intraday trade data for WIN and WDO and leading equities, and even moderate volumes of order‑book snapshots, over multi‑year horizons, but event‑level order‑book data for many years could approach capacity limits. As noted earlier, rough approximations suggest that daily bars and one‑minute bars consume negligible space, while tick‑by‑tick trade data for two futures contracts over a year may occupy on the order of tens of gigabytes, and full order‑book event streams may reach hundreds of gigabytes. The pipeline should therefore prioritise efficient storage formats such as columnar file formats (for example, Parquet) and robust indexing strategies to enable fast querying.

For daily and intraday bar data, partitioning by instrument and month, and storing data in compressed columnar files, will minimise storage overhead and enable efficient filtering by date and instrument. For tick‑by‑tick trade data, similar partitioning and compression strategies can be applied, with additional indexing on timestamp and instrument codes to accelerate range queries. Order‑book data, particularly if event‑level, benefits from partitioning by instrument and day, as queries often focus on single trading sessions. Given that the pipeline is intended for research rather than live trading, query latency may be less critical than overall storage efficiency and data integrity.

The pipeline must also manage memory usage carefully when analysing order‑book data. Even if disk space is ample, loading entire months of event‑level order‑book data into memory may be infeasible. Streaming analyses or chunked processing, along with summarisation routines that derive features such as order‑flow imbalance, price impact or microstructure variables from raw events, can mitigate memory constraints. For example, a common research approach is to aggregate order‑book events into time‑bars (e.g., one‑second snapshots) or volume‑bars, thus reducing event counts significantly while retaining key dynamical features.
